### FIRST REPORT OF HUMAN MYIASIS IN GOIA ´S STATE, BRAZIL: FREQUENCY OF DIFFERENT TYPES OF MYIASIS, THEIR VARIOUS ETIOLOGICAL AGENTS, AND ASSOCIATED FACTORS
Ly F. Fernandes, Fabiana C. Pimenta*, and Fernando F. Fernandes † Universidade Federal de Goia ´s (UFG), Faculdade de Medicina da UFG. Departamento de Clı ´nica Ciru ´rgica do Hospital das Clı ´nicas da UFG. Servic ¸o de Cirurgia Vascular. Primeira Avenida S/N Setor Universita ´rio CEP 74605-020, Goia ˆnia-Goia ´s, Brazil. e-mail: lyffreitas@gmail.com ABSTRACT: The objective of this study was to show which species of flies are responsible for human myiasis in the Brazilian state of Goia ´s and to determine the frequency of cases. Patients at the Clinical Hospital of the Federal University of Goia ´s (UFG) were examined, and any fly larvae found in their wounds were collected for taxonomic identification. First instar larvae were observed using light microscopy; second and third instars were examined using stereoscopy. The following screwworm flies were observed, in decreasing order of prevalence: Cochliomyia hominivorax, Sarcodexia lambens, Dermatobia hominis, Chrysomya albiceps, Chrysomya megacephala, Lucilia cuprina, and Eristalis tenax. Myiasis was most frequent in the legs in adults, male patients, elderly people, and people of reproductive age. It was lowest in children, females, and patients with neurologic or psychiatric disorders. Frequency was high in patients living in low socioeconomic conditions with poor personal hygiene. Education and sanitation measures are needed to counteract this situation.

Myiases (derived from the Greek term myia, meaning fly) are infestations of animal and human tissue by larvae of Diptera. Certain species of dipterans, particularly those of Muscomorpha, lay eggs or larvae on food, open wounds, skin, or mucosal tissue. Their larvae feed on live or necrotic tissues of the host or on foodstuffs (Millikan, 1999;Linhares, 2005;Nascimento et al., 2005).

Myiasis can be classified as obligatory, facultative, or pseudomyiasis, depending on the biology of the fly. Obligatory myiases are caused by larvae that develop while feeding in live tissues of the host. These species are the most harmful to mammalian hosts, including humans. The facultative myiases are caused by species whose larvae usually develop in decomposing organic matter (free-living larvae), but which also can act as parasites when developing in necrotic tissues of hosts. In pseudomyiasis, the eggs or larvae are accidentally swallowed with ingested food or contaminated water (Millikan, 1999;Linhares, 2005). The types of myiasis can also be clinically defined by the site of infection or affected tissues as follows: traumatic (or wound) myiasis; myiasis of the mouth (palate and periodontal), nose, nasopharynx, and accessory sinuses; lung myiasis; aural myiasis; ocular myiasis (internal and external); myiasis of the anal region and vagina; myiasis of the bladder and urinary tract; furuncular dermal or subdermal myiasis; creeping dermal eruption (cutaneous); and enteric myiasis (gastrointestinal, gastric, or intestinal) (James, 1947). The fly groups that are most commonly responsible for obligatory myiasis include Cuterebrinae (Fernandes and Linardi, 2002;Fernandes, Chiarini-Garcia, and Linardi, 2004), Sarcophagidae, and a few species of Calliphoridae that cause cutaneous and subdermal disease, mainly C. hominivorax in the New World (Fernandes, Pimenta, and Linardi, 2004) and Chrysomya bezziana in the Old World, as well as Gasterophilinae in the digestive tract (mainly of equids) (Fernandes et al., 2005), Oestrinae in nasopharyngeal cavities (mainly of sheep), and Muscidae (Philornis sp.) in birds. Among those that cause accidental myiasis are Syrphidae (E. tenax), Stratiomyidae (Hermetia illuscens), and Tephritidae (Ceratitis capitata). Several species of Calliphoridae, Sarcophagidae, and Muscidae are known to cause facultative myiasis (Guimara ˜es and Papavero, 1999).

The importance of myiasis depends on the tissues attacked. It can be a serious problem to the host, especially when it occurs in tissue with no space for expansion due to proximity of bone, i.e., the eyes, nose, face, and ears (Millikan, 1999). However, in some instances, myiasis can be beneficial to the host, since the larvae of some species produce lysozymes that kill certain bacteria. These are then ingested, resulting in cleaning and digestion of necrotic tissues. Other species are able to stimulate angiogenesis through the movements of the larvae on the surface of the wound, with this being an accepted method of alternative healing therapy (Lemos and Terra, 1991;Ito et al., 1995;Millikan, 1999).

The absence of knowledge on the frequency or prevalence of the different kinds of myiases in the Brazilian state of Goia ´s State motivated the present study. Our study was designed to identify the etiologic agents of these myiases, to examine the different behaviors and biology of Muscomorpha species, and to develop new and more efficient strategies of control and prophylaxis against the flies that cause myiasis.

### MATERIALS AND METHODS
Patients arrived for treatment at the emergency room of the Hospital das Clı ´nicas (HC) of the UFG, the most important, and only public, teaching hospital in the state, between February 2005 and August 2006. Those diagnosed with myiasis were selected for the present study. Lesions were treated and necrotic tissue and/or larvae found in patients were removed on admission. The wounds were washed with saline solution, and all standard care procedures were employed. Dipteran larvae from patients were collected in 2 polyethylene disposable containers, 1 containing 10% formaldehyde solution and the other empty.

The containers were taken to the Laboratory of Medical and Veterinary Artropodology of the Institute of Tropical Pathology and Public Health at UFG for identification of the larvae according to taxonomic keys (James, 1947;Zumpt, 1965;Teskey, 1981;James, 1985;Leite and Lopes, 1989;Pape, 1996;Queiroz et al., 1997;Guimara ˜es and Papavero, 1999;Wells et al., 1999;Amorim and Ribeiro, 2001). The larvae were then isolated until pupation was complete. Larval instars were maintained in Biological Oxygen Demand incubators at 27 Ϯ 1 C, (relative humidity) RH Ն80% under a 12:12 hr photoperiod, according to Fernandes et al. (2002), and fed with beef until pupation occurred. The newly pupated flies were then identified (James, 1985;Guimara ˜es and Papavero, 1999).

Patients and their relatives were interviewed to obtain information regarding socioeconomic levels and acquisition of the myiases, as well as to obtain permission to photograph and treat the wounds

### RESULTS
Sixty-six patients were seen and treated between February 2005 and August 2006 at HC/UFG. Myiasis occurred in patients of both sexes aged 2-91 yr, although it was more common in males. Both furuncle and multiple forms were seen.

The myiases were subsequently classified: 47 (71.2%) were found to be obligatory, 16 (24.2%) facultative, and 2 (3.0%) both obligatory and facultative. Pseudomyiasis was observed in only 1 (1.5%) patient, who vomited larvae that were subsequently identified as E. tenax (Syrphidae) (Fig. 1).

The frequency of larval fly species identified in human wounds is shown in Figure 2. These included C. hominivorax (41/66, 62.1%), S. lambens (8/66, 12.1%), D. hominis (7/66, 10.6%), C. albiceps (4/66, 6.1%), C. megacephala (3/66, 4.5%), L. cuprina (1/66, 1.5%), and E. tenax (1/66, 1.5%). Double infection occurred twice (3.0%), i.e., C. hominivorax and C. albiceps occurred together once, as did C. hominivorax and S. lambens.

Third instar larvae were the most common, occurring in 66.7% (44/66) of the cases, with first and second larval instars representing only 9.1% of the total (6/66). All instars were present in 24.2% (16/66) of the cases. Lesions harbored 1-261 larvae.

The interaction between myiases, disease, and predisposing factors is shown in Figure 3. Myiasis was the result of wounds from various causes and necrosis was associated with myiasis in 19 (28.8%) of the cases. Other associated conditions included diabetes (15 cases, 22.7%); neuropathic ulcers caused by diabetes, alcoholism, and leprosy (14 cases, 21.2%); varicose ulcers and sequelae of leprosy (both in 7 cases, 10.6%); activities such as trips to forests, farms, or for fishing (6 cases, 9.1%) (D. hominis); alcoholism (5 cases, 7.6%); and neurologic pathologies (4 cases, 6.1%). These conditions also included 1 case of a sequel to cerebral ischemia in childbirth, 2 cases of strokes resulting from cerebral vascular accident, and another case as a sequel of a rote cerebral aneurysm. Infections were associated with other lesions such as traumatic lesions (3 cases, 4.5%); erysipelas (2 cases, 3.0%); osteomyelitis, impetigo, pediculosis, and otitis (4 cases, 6.1%); and rhinitis (2 cases, 3.0%).

Other factors included tumors (cancer) (3 cases, 4.5%) (breast, ovary, bladder); visual deficiency (1 case, 1.5%); drug dependence; surgical wounds such as tracheotomies (1 case, 1.5%); mycoses and oral breathing (2 cases each, 3.0%); lack of hygiene (4 cases, 6.1%); and schizophrenia (1 case, 1.5%). Factors indirectly associated included ischemia in 11 (16.7%) cases, i.e., ischemic ulcers as a result of chronic arterial obstruction caused by arteriosclerosis and peripheral arteriopathies.

Some cases showed arterial hypertension (3 cases, 4.5%), as well as cardiac disease and hyperuricemia (1 case each, 1.5%). Unidentified factors were noted in 5 cases (7.6%).

The patients were aged 2-91 yr, including 37 (56.1%) of working age (23-67 yr). Eighteen cases occurred in elderly patients (27.3%) and 11 in children (16.7%); prevalence was higher in adults (55 cases, 83.33%) than in children and nearly twice as frequent in males (43 cases, 65.1%) compared with females (23 cases, 34.8%). Myiasis was also more frequent in people of working age (37 cases, 56.1%) than in elderly, retired citizens (18 cases, 27.3%).

The frequency of myiasis treated were classified in relation to the localization of the lesions (clinical classification). Thirty cases (30/66, 45.4%) were observed in the feet, most commonly those of diabetics (11/30, 36.7%), and legs (14/66, 21.2%), including 3 diabetics (3/14, 21.4%). Others sites included the scalp (9/66, 13.6%), ears (3/66, 4.5%), retro-auricular (2/66, 3.0%), palate (1/66, 1.5%), tongue and gums (1/66, 1.5%), perorbital (1/66, 1.5%), nasal (2/66, 3.0%), tracheal (1/66, 1.5%), breast (1/66, 1.5%), and digestive tract (1/66, 1.5%). Multiple myiases occurred mainly on the legs (44/66, 66.7%), usually due to lack of care and parental attention, poor hygiene, and improper bandaging and cleansing of wounds. These factors produced odors that attracted flies. Several cases of children with multiple scalp myiasis were associated with pediculosis and impetigo.

Most clinical cases observed were associated with the lack of care and hygiene and low socioeconomic conditions (90.9%). Most of the patients earned the Brazilian minimum wage as their sole family income, with only 9.1% (6/66) receiving a slightly higher salary.

These myiases were in some cases related to mental, neurologic, or psychiatric deficiency or to ulcers of the legs due to deep vein thrombosis, complications of diabetes, bedridden patients, and children.

The interaction between climatic parameters and myiasis distribution found in the present study is shown in Figure 4.

The chi-square test at P Ͻ 0.05 was used to show that associated environmental factors, necrosis, varicose veins, neuropathies, diabetes, sequelae of hanseniasis, alcoholism, osteomyelitis, erysipelas, mycoses, lack of hygiene, trauma, cancer, oral breathing, visual deficiency, drug dependence, tracheotomy, psychiatric and neurologic deficiencies, otitis, rhinitis, pe- diculosis, and impetigo (chi-square ϭ 0.001), as well as age (chi-square ϭ 0.006), all influenced the occurrence of the diagnosed myiases significantly. There was no significant difference in myiasis occurrence between the sexes (chi-square ϭ 0.218).

### DISCUSSION
Among our group of patients, myiasis was most commonly found in poor and malnourished people who lived alone or were cared for by relatives who had little concern for their wellbeing. These results are consistent with those of studies conducted by Durighetto et al. (1995), which associated the appearance of myiases with improper care, substandard living and hygienic conditions, malnutrition, alcoholism, mental retardation, senility, trauma, paralysis, oral breathing during sleep, and the presence of necrosis or infection; all of these conditions can be attractive to flies (Durighetto et al., 1995).

Although other studies have shown myiasis to occur in people with good living conditions, high income, and education, most of those cases occurred among patients in intensive care units (Beckendorf et al., 2002;Chigusa, Kirinoki, and Matsuda, 2005;Chigusa, Nemoto et al., 2005). These individuals did not receive special attention for wound dressing; loss of motility and consciousness, puncture wounds, draining of abscesses, assisted breathing and tracheal tubes, and other injuries resulting from the continuous exposure of mucosae to secretions seem to favor the occurrence of these pathologies.

Recent studies indicate that D. hominis and C. hominivorax are the predominant agents of human myiases in the New Word (Nascimento et al., 2005), as well as of human and domestic or wild animals worldwide (Hall and Wall, 1995;Guimara ˜es and Papavero, 1999). The latter species was also predominant in the myiases diagnosed in our study (Fig. 2).

Myiases generally caused by D. hominis include a furuncular skin variety in which the larva is commonly known in Brazil as a ''berne.'' Furuncular myiasis observed in the present study always resulted from trips to farms, contact with forests, or fishing trips. This form occurs mainly among inhabitants of forested area habitats where flies that act as phoretic hosts of the egg are abundant. These hosts include day-flying mosquitoes and zoophilic flies (Culicidae, Simuliidae, some members of Tabanidae, Fanniidae, Anthomyiidae, Muscidae, Sarcophagidae, Calliphoridae) (Guimara ˜es and Papavero, 1999). These phoretic dipterans are detected by female D. hominis on their hosts by olfactory sensilla, microscopic sensorial organs that contain pores for penetration of the odors and are associated with neurons in the hypodermis (Halberg and Hansson, 1999). These neurons are located mainly in the antennae (Fernandes et al., 2002;Fernandes, Pimenta, and Linardi, 2004) and mouthparts of D. hominis (Fernandes and Linardi, 2002). Other sensilla of the gustatory type (also known as contact-chemoreceptor sensilla) are used at close distances for locating phoretic dipterans, sexual partners, foods, and oviposition sites. These sensilla contain an apical pore and are present mainly around the labella, genae, ovipositor, and tarsi of the Muscomorpha (Fernandes and Linardi, 2002;Fernandes, Chiarini-Garcia, and Linardi, 2004;Fernandes et al., 2005).

The absence of larvae of the Neotropical calliphorid Cochliomyia macellaria can be explained by its increasing rarity as a result of competition with introduced Old World species with superior biotic potential (Guimara ˜es and Papavero, 1999). These include C. albiceps, which was collected during the present study (Fig. 2).

Diabetics and people with sequelae of leprosy, alcoholism with neuropathy, and digit wounds, as well as those with hyperkeratosic lesions of the skin, have a greater tendency to suffer infestations, due to their lack of sensitivity and the higher probability of having lesions. Venous ulcers (Fig. 5A), ischemia, and necrosis (Fig. 5B) are often associated with myiases.

Patients with neurologic and psychiatric pathologies such as strokes, medullar lesions, and physical deficiencies, as well as bedridden individuals, presented lesions with higher numbers of larvae in unusual regions, such as the periorbital and periodontal regions and tongue. Although a higher prevalence of myiases was expected in patients with neurologic sequelae, they occurred more frequently in professionally active people.

Most infestations, however, occurred in people with inadequate hygiene and care rather than in bedridden individuals. There was a higher prevalence in people with lower economic conditions, which can be related to inadequate care. Many more cases were recorded in patients with low socioeconomic levels, substandard living conditions, a lack of hygiene or housing, inadequate care for lesions, or daily failure to change dressings and maintain cleanliness of the wounds. Larvae were observed to pass through dressings wet with secretion (Fig. 5F).

Myiasis was also associated with pediculosis, impetigo, and parental negligence of children who were often taken care of by other children (Figs. 5C-D). It is very important to change wound dressings daily or whenever the lesion becomes wet with secretion to prevent the attraction of flies. Flies are also attracted to odor associated with chronic otitis media. Although myiasis is generally very harmful, in one case of necrotic erysipelas, infestation with L. cuprina larvae (Fig. 5E) helped cleanse the wound and promoted good granulation of the subdermal tissues. This suggests that further studies should be made of larval use in wound therapy.

Temperature appeared to exert minor influence, with little variation observed in the evolution of cases. However, tropical climates are presumably favorable to the development of Muscomorpha species throughout the year. Relative humidity and rainfall positively influenced the occurrence of myiasis from November of 2005 to March 2006 (Fig. 4).

Although the present study was conducted in the most important public hospital of the Goia ´s State, we believe that it does not reflect the true prevalence of human myiasis in this state. The findings are in agreement with those of Nascimento et al. (2005) in discounting the view prevalent in the literature that myiasis is rare in humans.

In the present study, underreporting of the disease was observed to be due to factors such as (1) myiasis not being an illness of obligatory registration; (2) general revulsion toward the patient by health professionals, leading to larvae and dressings being discarded without further examination; (3) health professionals judging myiasis to be a disease of minor importance; (4) domestic, empirical treatment of patients by family members; and (5) treatment of cases in health facilities close to where patients reside.

Other difficulties in determining the true prevalence include lack of computerized data entry due to scarcity of resources and staff, inability to carry out retrospective studies, and ignorance on the part of many health professionals regarding the different types of myiasis and their characteristics, resulting in the lack of detailed reports. However, on rare occasions, physicians may send samples of larvae to entomology laboratories specializing in Muscomorpha taxonomy and identification.

The present study is the first to deal with the occurrence of human myiasis in Goia ´s. We hope to increase awareness of health professionals as to the importance of recording cases of myiasis and identifying the species involved. These data may indicate which treatment or prophylactic measures should be used for each patient and help in the design of control strategies.
