The purpose of our study was to evaluate the larvicidal efficacy of nitenpyram for the treatment of screwworm myiasis caused by Cochliomyia hominivorax in naturally infested dogs. Seven Beagle dogs presenting with myiasis were treated with nitenpyram twice at an interval of 6 h. The animals received dosages between 1.43 and 4.42 mg/kg body weight of nitenpyram in each given treatment. The criteria used to determine the drug efficacy were larval expulsion and fall. These were quantified at 15 min intervals within the first hour followed by 2, 3, 4, 6, and 18 h post-treatment. The highest larval expulsion happened between 1 and 2 h post-treatment. The spontaneous larval expulsion percentages were 86% and 94.11% for 6 and 18 h after the first treatment, respectively. Larvae were expelled until 18 h post-treatment. After this period the remaining dead larvae were mechanically removed. Nitenpyram showed 100% efficacy on the treatment of myiasis by C. hominivorax in naturally infested dogs.

### Introduction
Cochliomyia hominivorax (Coquerel, 1858) (Diptera: Calliphoridae) is popularly known as the New World screwworm fly. Historically, its geographical distribution extends throughout Tropical and Subtropical America, from the Southern United States to the North of Chile. Due to its wide host range, this fly has been considered a major cause of obligatory myiasis (Rawlins, 1985). Recently, this dipteran was included in the list of Global Early Systems for major Animal Diseases, including zoonoses of the World Health Organization (WHO, 2007).

Infestation in domestic dog has been reported in many Neotropical countries, such as Brazil and Argentina, and it is considered a common problem in small animal practice. Its incidence is higher during warm weather months (Marluis et al., 1994;Cramer-Ribeiro et al., 2002).

Severe infestations can lead to death if not diagnosed and treated in an early stage (Guimarães et al., 2001). The diagnosis is based on the characteristic offensive smell of infested animals wounds, and identification of the maggots (Mendes- de-Almeida et al., 2007).

Some divergences have been mentioned in reference to predilection areas in dogs. The mostly cited affected regions were perinasal, periocular, peribucal, perianal and genital (Marcondes, 2001) or auricular (Cramer-Ribeiro et al., 2002).

In small animal practice, ivermectin and pyrethrin or pyrethroids sprays are commonly used for the treatment of screwworm myiasis (Scott et al., 2001). Despite the lack of information in the literature, the use of unguents or sprays containing organophosphates or carbamates are known to be still largely employed.

Nitenpyram is a highly water-soluble molecule from the class of neonicotinoids. Once orally administered it is rapidly absorbed via host blood, and also rapidly excreted; and therefore is considered of low toxicity for mammals. It leads to insect death by nervous transmission interference (Schenker et al., 2001;Scott et al., 2002;Hovda and Hoser, 2002). This drug has been used for flea control in dogs and cats, usually associated to insect growth regulators (Dobson et al., 2000). Additionally, it has been routinely used in small animal practice in the treatment screwworm infestations, usually with a single-dose, following the recommended dosage for flea control (Machado and Rodrigues, 2002).

The purpose of the present study is to evaluate the larvicidal efficacy of nitenpyram for the treatment of screwworm myiasis caused by C. hominivorax in naturally infested dogs.

### Materials and methods
Seven Beagle dogs (six males and 1 female), aging from 9 months to 6 years, and weighting from 7.9 to 13.2 kg were used as subjects. The animals were maintained by the Kennel of the Laboratory of Experimental Chemotherapy in Veterinary Parasitology from the Universidade Federal Rural do Rio de Janeiro, Southeastern Brazil. All dogs were naturally infested by C. hominivorax larvae, constituting cases of primary myiasis. The lesions were distributed in different body areas: mentum (2), preputial (1), left ear (1), right ear (1), interdigital (1) and perianal (1) and appeared during different seasons of the year.

Myiasis detections were made through visual observation of characteristic injuries containing larvae in addition to their putrid odor. Dogs were medicated orally with nitenpyram tablets (Capstar ® , Novartis Animal Health), following recommended protocol for flea control. A second treatment was administered 6 h after the first administration. The animals received dosages between 1.43 and 4.42 mg/kg body weight of nitenpyram per treatment.

The dogs were maintained in individual cages measuring 70 cm × 70 cm × 70 cm, with removable trays placed in the bottom. Expelled larvae present on these trays were periodically collected and quantified every 15 min during the first hour and then at 2, 3, 4, 6 and 18 h post-treatment. Maggots were taxonomically identified as C. hominivorax second and third instar larvae, according to Guimarães and Papavero (1999). The remaining larvae were mechanically removed with the aid of surgical tweezers and then counted. Later, the wounds were cleaned and treated using antiseptic solution and topical healing ointment. Systemic antibiotic and anti-inflammatory treatment were given.

The criteria for drug evaluation were the expulsion and consequent fall of larvae from the lesion. Larval expulsion percentage was calculated by the formula [(number of expelled larvae of each animal)/(sum of expelled and mechanically collected larvae of each animal) × 100]. The percentages of expelled larvae per observational period, considering all animals, were calculated by the following formula: [(total of expelled larvae by each time)/(sum of expelled and mechanically collected larvae)].

The second treatment was given 6 h after the first nitempyran administration, as an attempt to kill the remaining active larvae infesting all dogs. We had 95.3% of expelled larvae in 18 h after the first treatment (Table 1).

Considering the total of expelled larvae for each evaluation time, the mean non-cumulative expulsion was 0%, 0% and 0.1% for 15, 30 and 45 min; and 4.2%, 41.4%, 19.1%, 8.2%, 17.1% and 9.8% for 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 18 h after the first treatment (Fig. 1).

The drug comprised a mean efficacy of 86% and 95.3%, respectively, for 6 and 18 h after the first treatment for the removal of C. hominivorax larvae. Since the remaining maggots were found dead, nitenpyram showed an overall larvicidal efficacy of 100%.

As stated by Hovda and Hoser (2002) nitenpyram reaches in 20 min a serum level sufficient to act against the insects. They also affirm that the maximum serum concentration occurs in 1.2 h post-ingestion which justifies the higher larval expulsion obtained between 1 and 2 h posttreatment in the current study (Fig. 1).

During the last decade, other insecticidal drugs have been used successfully for the prevention and treatment of screwworm myiasis in different animal species. Conversely, the subcutaneous application is painful and the 48 h period is considered extremely long as the larvae permanence in the wound could aggravate the damage. Machado and Rodrigues (2002) used a single-dose treatment with nitenpyram for naturally screwworm infested dogs and found that all the maggots were killed 24 h after treatment in 94.1% of the subjects. The double nitenpyram treatment used in the present study might have assisted in the achievement of 100% larvicidal activity, but we cannot assure whether or not it was essential for this. In general, an effective and rapid larval expulsion is desired once it will reduce the extent of tissue damage provoked by larval action and certainly benefit infested animals. Consequently, repeated treatments could be recommended according to the level, location, and severity of infestation.

As shown by Nesbitt and Ackerman (1996), the curative treatment of either cutaneous and cavitary myiasis in dogs has the following indications: mechanical removal of maggots, lesion cleansing, hair plucking, and in some cases the patient sedation is necessary. Usually, wound debridement is required, as well as combined anti-inflammatory and antibiotic protocols. Additionally, repellent and larvicidal products are commonly used. Antiparasitic products such as nitenpyram should be largely employed, especially for the treatment of massive or barely accessible infestations, consisting in a useful and efficacious tool for veterinarians.

### Conclusion
Oral treatment with nitenpyram promoted hastily larval expulsion when administered to naturally C. hominivorax infested dogs, being considered an effective therapeutical option. This fast and spontaneous expulsion of maggots is a desirable factor for drugs intended for myiasis treatment in small animal practice.
