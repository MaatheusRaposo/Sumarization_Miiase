Abstract Cases of myiasis in man associated with preexisting lesions are quite common. However, in the forensic field, certain behavioral habits related to insects may be useful to ratify mistreatment. This is a register of a case of oral myiasis by Cochliomyia hominivorax (Diptera: Calliphoridae), an obligate parasite of warm-blooded animals, in a 95-year-old man with Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease. The identification of immatures removed (n087) was based on their morphological characteristics. Considering the developmental time and length of the maggots, it was possible to estimate an infestation of at least 143 h. Undoubtedly, the poor state of health was one of the cofactors for the infestation; however, the long time of infestation observed denotes negligence in the conduct of patient care. In terms of prophylaxis, it would be recommended that individuals who need special care must have access to more comprehensive physical examinations and periodicals, as well as hygienic conditions should be meticulously observed. Additionally, the immatures causing myiasis after being removed must be stored and forwarded for analysis, since their identification can be crucial to determine the exact time and circumstances of an infestation, and consequently its pathogenesis. If we consider the aggressiveness of C. hominivorax, a fast and reliable diagnosis becomes even more important for an adequate treatment. Finally, it is expected that this study encourages the health care professionals to improve the treatment of incapable patients.

Myiasis is the designation given to the infestation of dipteran larvae in vertebrates that, at least for a certain period, feed on their alive or dead tissues, their liquid body substances, or their ingested food (Zumpt 1965). Thus, fly larvae which complete their cycle, or at least part of it, in or on the body of a vertebrate host is classified as causing myiasis (Linhares and Thyssen 2007).

According to Patton (1922), considering the life cycle of flies and parasite-host relationship, myiasis can be classified into primary, secondary, or accidental. In the primary type, the larvae are obligate parasites, once they develop exclusively in or on the body of an often specific host, feeding on living tissue. Immatures responsible for secondary myiasis usually develop in organic substrates of animal origin in decay, but eventually they can attack and feed on necrotic tissue from preexisting wounds or ulcers in living hosts, so they are also called facultative parasites. In accidental myiasis, eggs or larvae reach the body opportunely, via natural orifices, traumatic wounds, or even the digestive tract when ingested with food and can cause some serious injuries if they survive.

Records of cases in domestic animals (e.g., see Lello et al. 1982;Cramer-Ribeiro et al. 2002) and humans associated with injuries resulting from chronic diseases, postoperative, or any other etiology are relatively common (e.g., Lukin 1989;Sherman 2000;Nascimento et al. 2005;Fonseca et al. 2007), since fetid odors or blood may attract insects that cause myiasis, most of them belonging to the family Calliphoridae (Diptera) (Guimarães and Papavero 1999).

Within the family Calliphoridae, Cochliomyia hominivorax (Cocquerel, 1858), originating in the New World, stands out for being an obligate parasite of warm-blooded animals, so it gains great economic importance, particularly with regard to livestock (Dear 1985;Hall and Wall 1995). Even though adults may be attracted to wounds or ulcers on the skin of the host, its larvae do not feed on necrotic material; when they invade natural orifices, it may result in cavitary myiasis of several types, which culminates in major tissue damage or even death of its hosts (Guimarães et al. 2001). They preferentially live in forested areas, but they come to open pastures looking for oviposition sites and they are capable of placing up to 400 eggs per oviposition (Guimarães and Papavero 1999).

In forensic entomology, the insects are subject of study for answering to inherent questions in legal investigations, and in some cases, behavioral habits associated to these organisms are useful to confirm mistreatment or neglect when dealing with disabled or invalid people (specially elderly, children, and declared invalid persons) such as registered by Benecke and Lessig (2001) and Benecke et al. (2004). Thus, the present study aimed to report a case of primary oral myiasis which evidences negligence in the care of an elder man.

The nursing staff of a private clinic of long permanence in São Paulo, State of São Paulo, Brazil, notified that a 95-year-old male patient with Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease was presenting strong discomfort and bad breath, and after spraying saline in the mouth region, they also noticed movement of larvae there. The clinical examination revealed no labial sealing, poor mobility of the tongue, gingivitis, and advanced periodontal disease, besides mouth breathing and fairly extensive necrotic lesion in the region of the hard palate with destruction of bone tissue and gingival (Fig. 1a, b). In the hospital environment and under local anesthesia, all periodontally compromised teeth of the patient's upper arch (n016) were extracted. Afterwards, the larvae (n087) concentrated between alveoli and tissues of the hard palate, identified as belonging to the species   C. hominivorax, were removed by curettage followed by osteoplasty.

The identification of immature was based on their external and internal morphological characteristics (Hall 1948): cylindrical, segmented, white yellow color body, and tracheas with strong pigmentation, visible until the third or fourth larval segment immediately preceding the end portion of the body (Fig. 2).

Considering the egg incubation period, the time of development, and larval length of the different larval stages in the host (Table 1), it was possible to estimate that the larvae removed-which had an average of 10 mm in length -had over 132 h of life. Even if it was not possible to determine the exact age of the immature-that were in intense feeding activity (Fig. 1c)-because of lack of more detailed data in the literature, we can surely state that the initial infestation occurred at least 143 h before the observation and encounter of the first immatures on the patient.

The precarious odontological state of the patient, whose family had been warned 9 months earlier, has contributed to the occurrence of infestation observed; undoubtedly, inadequate measures of hygiene and lack of specific care allowed the immature to remain on site and cause the injuries observed. In other studies about occurrence of myiasis in hospitals, Lukin (1989), Çiftçioglu et al. (1997), andNascimento et al. (2005) pointed out the fact that unfortunately reports related to patients with some type of physical and mental weakness are recurrent, denoting the negligence in dealing with them. Moreover, Sherman (2000) mentioned that it is possible that nosocomial myiasis occasionally may need to be considered a risk of certain underlying diseases (i.e., impaired consciousness, purulent infection, or malodorous secretions) and/or their treatments (i.e., sedatives, drainage tubes, and respiratory and gastrointestinal intubation).

Taking into account the gravity in which may reach a myiasis case, specially the primary type, it is expected that the work of odontology professionals combined with the entomological findings here described can lead to awareness on the consequences of the misconduct of those responsible for the care of disabled or invalid. Also, it is worth noting that myiasis are always embarrassing and disgusting for both the patient and the health professional that mediates the treatment (Shinohara et al. 2004), as well illustrated in this current report. Although it is not possible to quantify the contribution of myiasis in this regard, the fact is that the patient had depression as soon as his teeth have been extracted and died a few months later.
