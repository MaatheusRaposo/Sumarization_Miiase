# Primary Myiasis in Dog Caused by Phaenicia eximia (Diptera: Calliphoridae) and Preliminary Mitochondrial DNA Analysis of the Species in Brazil
A Azeredo-Espin
N Madeira

Published on February 19, 2026

A case of primary myiasis in a dog caused by Phaenicia eximia (Robineau-Desvoidy) in Brazil is presented. A young and healthy female dog, Canis familiaris, =10 d old and still under maternal care, was found to have several eggs and 1st instars larvae in its abdomen and urogenital regions. Samples were collected in Campinas, Sao Paulo, and transferred to the laboratory for rearing and identification. A comparative analysis of the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) with 12 restriction enzymes in 2 sampled populations of P. eximia collected in different hosts (live dog and bovine carcass) and in the same locality revealed that 4, EcoRI, EcoRV, HaeWl, and Mspl were suitable for detecting mtDNA markers in the 2 populations.

THE FAMILY CALLIPHORIDAE contains many species of flies who display diverse larval feeding strategies. A survey of the literature concerned with larval trophies (e.g., Fuller 1934, James 1947, Hall 1948, Aldrich 1961, Oldroyd 1964, Cole and Schlinger 1969, Hall and Wall 1995) reveals 2 general groupings of resource exploitation. To group 1 belong species whose larvae use living tissue of mammals, Dirds, amphibians, and a variety of invertebrates as a food source. Members of group 2 are primarily necrophagous, feeding on the tissues of carrion (Norris 1965).

Ecological studies have been made of 2 main types of myiasis-producing blow flies: the sheep blow flies (fleece worms), Phaenicia cuprina (Wiedemann) and P. sericata (Meigen), and the wound myiasis fly (screwworm), Cochliomyia hominivorax (Coquerel), and its Old World screwworm counterpart Chrysomya bezziana (Villeneuve). In Australia, P. cuprina known as Lucilia cuprina, has been reported causing primary myiasis in sheep (Waterhouse and Paramanov 1950).

Sheep blow flies occur early in the succession of blow fly species at carcasses, but are highly susceptible to competition or predation by species arriving later. Frequently, few complete their development (Holdaway 1930, Fuller 1934, Waterhouse 1947, Cragg 1955). An exception occurs when the animal suffers from myiasis before death (Water-house 1947, Cragg 1955). Under these conditions, larvae, which are advanced at the time of death, are able to complete their development without risk of elimination by competition or predation from other species. Norris (1990) suggest that the competition with other species for carcasses as breeding site is the underlying cause for behavioral changes leading to the exploitation of living tissues of animals by P. cuprina.

Phaenicia eximia (Robineau-Desvoidy) is a Neartic and Neotropical species frequently found in rural, urban, and forest habitats in Brazil, Peru, and Argentina (Ferreira 1978, Linhares 1981, Madeira et al. 1982, Baumgartner and Greenberg 1985, Mariluis et al. 1990) and in forest in the United States (Godard and Lago 1985). The adults of P. eximia are attracted to a wide variety of substrates for food and reproduction, such as garbage, carcasses, and human feces (Mendes and Linhares 1993). This species has been implicated as a mechanical vector of human and animal diseases (Murray and Thompson 1976). In Brazil, there is only 1 reported case of P. eximia causing primary myiases in a cat, Felis domesticus (Madeira et al. 1989).

At the genetic level, investigations of natural populations of P. eximia have been limited to the analysis of the population dynamics and behavior of caliphorids, including P. eximia (Linhares 1981, Madeira et al. 1982, Ferreira 1983, Madeira 1985, Wiegand et al. 1991, Paraluppi 1992, Mendes and Linhares 1993). These studies have shown that P. eximia is primarily necrophagous, feeding on the tissues of carrion. P. eximia is the most abundant blow fly in Curitiba,Brazil,representing 69% of Vol. 33,no. 5 the total (Ferreira 1978), in Minas Gerais it is only 18% (Madeira et al. 1982). Prado and Guimaraes (1982), suggest that P. eximia is being displaced by the newly introduced Chrysomya spp. in some areas of Brazil, for example, Ferreira (1982) reported a decline of P. eximia from 17 to 8% of total blow flies in 3 yr in Goiania, following the establishment of C. putoria. Price (1980) hypothesized that many parasite species have locally inbred populations, with large genetic differentiation among populations from different geographic regions or hosts. Remarkably little is known about the genetic structure of blowflies and about between-population differentiation. Most genetic work on differentiation in insects species has focused on searching for genetic markers, such as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and nuclear DNA restriction fragment variants, to distinguish diversity within and between populations (Nadler 1990, Thompson andLymbery 1990). The analysis of mtDNA with restriction enzymes has proven to be a useful method in investigations of genetic varibility and evolution within species as well as the systematics of closely related species. The advantages and applications of studies have been reviewed by Wilson et al. (1985), Avise et al. (1987), andMoritz et al. (1987). In the Calliphoridae family, restriction endonucleases of mtDNA (restriction fragment length polymorphism) has been used to analyze the genetic variation in natural populations of C. hominivorax, from North, Central, and South America and North Africa (Roehrdanz and Johnson 1988, Roehrdanz 1989, Taylor et al. 1991, Azeredo-Espin 1993, Infante 1994, Infante and Azeredo-Espin 1995) and to characterize the organization of mitochondrial genome of the black blow fly, Phormia regina (Meigen) (Goldenthal et al. 1991).

We have chosen restriction analysis of mtDNA as a tool to examine the population genetics of P. eximia because it may be more sensitive in detecting population subdivision because of its haploid copy number, maternal inheritance, and lack of recombination (Avise et al. 1979, Wilson et al. 1985).

In the current study, we report a case of primary myiasis in a dog caused by P. eximia, in Campinas, Sao Paulo, and provide a preliminary characterization of mtDNA and restriction fragment patterns with 12 restriction enzymes collected from populations of P. eximia collected in the same locality but in 2 different hosts.

### Materials and Methods
A young and healthy female dog, Canis familiaris, -10 d old, still under maternal care, was found infested with eggs and 1st instars located in its abdomen and urogenital regions. The larvae (n = 25) and eggs (n = 40) were collected in a veterinary Hospital in Campinas, Sao Paulo, and transferred to a plastic box with fresh ground meat and sawdust. This rearing medium was changed every 24 h, until pupariation. The cage was maintained in the laboratory at 25 ± 1°C, 70% RH, and a photoperiod of 12:12 (L:D) h. Adults were used for identification. Females were allowed to oviposit on a fresh mouse carcass in a petri dish inside the cage. Eggs were then transferred to a plastic box containing mouse carcass. Mouse carcasses were replaced every 24 h, until pupation. Adult flies were also collected directly on a bovine carcass (n = 35), near slaughter houses in Campinas, and maintained in the laboratory under the same conditions.

Mitochondrial DNA Isolation. Fresh pupae (n = 20) of each of the 2 samples were used to purify mtDNA as described by Azeredo-Espin et al. (1991). One individual pupa was gently homogenized in a 15 ml Corex tube with 1 ml of griding buffer (10 mM TRIS, 660 mM NaCl, 300 mM sucrose, 10 mM EDTA). After this, 1 ml of lysis buffer was added (300 mM TRIS, 40 mM SDS, 20 mM EDTA) and 0.7% of DEPC. The resulting lysate was extracted once each with phenol, phenol: chloroform : isoamilic alcohol, and chloroform:isoamilic alcohol and centrifuged at 3,000 X g. The final supernatant was then ethanol precipitated at 20°C for 2 h and spun at 10,000 X g to pellet the DNA. The pellet was dried under vacuum and resuspended in an appropriate amount of 1 X TE.

Mitochondrial DNA Restriction Analysis. mtDNA was digested with restriction endonucleases according to manufacture's specifications (Bethesda Research Laboratories, Bethesda, MD). The endonucleases used were. Hindlll, EcoRI, Eco RV, Haelll, Msvl, Xho I, Xba I, Sst I, Cla I, BamHl, Pvu I, ana Pst I. After digestion, fragments were separated on 1% agarose gels and stained with Ethidium Bromide (0.1 g/ml). Phage A DNA digested with Hindlll and <f> X174 digested with Haelll (BRL) were used as size standards.

Pure C. hominivorax mtDNA for use as a probe was obtained from pupae of an isofemale laboratory strain started from a field population (Ca sample) (Azeredo-Espin 1993, Infante 1994, Infante and Azeredo-Espin 1995). MtDNA was obtained through the MIM (mitochondrial isolation medium) technique according to Azeredo-Espin et al. (1991), and purified by using 2 subsequent equilibrium centrifugations in CsCl.

To determine which fragments present on the gels were mtDNA and to detect small bands (0.5 kb), the fragments were transferred to nylon membranes (Hybond) through Southern Blot (Maniatis et al. 1989) and hybridized with the whole mtDNA of C. hominivorax, which was nick-translated to yield a 32 P-labeled probe. Hybridization was carried out under standard conditions (Maniatis et al. 1989). Visualization of mtDNA fragments was conducted using autoradiography based on Moritz et al. (1987).

The functional division of Calliphoridae, responsible for myiasis, may also reflect the evolution of the parasitic habit. Generalized free-living saprophagous feeders, such as calliphorids, which may occasionally act as agents of myiasis in wounded, dying, or otherwise clinically predisposed animals, may have formed die ancestral origins of the parasitic habit. These then gave rise to facultative ectoparasites, attracted to skin soiled by feces, bacterial infection, and suppurating wounds, such as JR cuprina, which behave as primary myiasis agents rather than saprophages. From this intermediate stage, the obligate parasites, for example, C. hominivorax and C. bezziana, developed (Zumpt 1965, Erzinclioglu 1989, Hall and Wall 1995). Hence, it would appear that these 2 screwworm species evolved in parallel, independently, in different parts of die world from necrophagous ancestor, with species of the genera such as Phoenicia representing a midpoint in diis development. Intermediate evolutionary stages within the genera of the 2 obligate parasites themselves are represented by C. macellaria, Chrysomya megacephala {¥.), C. putoria (Wiedemann), and C. albiceps (Wiedemann).

If the myiasis habit of Calliphoridae evolved from saprophagy, then it probably involved a change in the timing of attack. Thus, the primitive behavior, shown by carrion-breeding species, Vol. 33, no. 5 changed from attack on stressed or dying animals to attack on healthy animals (i.e., from vertebrate saprophage to feeder on vertebrate wounds, to obligate cause of vertebrate wounds).

The myiasis habit of the obligate ectoparasites may be a well-established life history strategy in evolutionary terms. In contrast, inter-and intraspecific variation in myiasis propensity and historical records of changes in myiasis incidence or species of importance provide a strong case to suggest that some of the species responsible for facultative myiasis may have coevolved with the domestication and spread of livestock and the intensification of their husbandry and may have expanded relatively rapidly and recently into the vacant niches so created. Possibly they were preadapted as general parasites of wildlife and so were able to take advantage of the spread of domestic, relatively nonresistant animals. This may be particularly true for species of the genus Phoenicia.

The factors inducing the P. eximia, which normally oviposits in carcasses, to infest a living animal remain unknown. In both reports of primary myiasis caused by P. eximia, the cat and dog were healthy and young 15 and 10 d old, respectively.

It seems probable that myiasis caused by P. eximia is similar to the competitive displacement of P. cuprina observed in Australia, where several species of primarily necrophagous flies have shown a capacity to establish cutaneous myiasis of sheep comparable with those of P. eximia. In various countries where sheep raising has developed, several species of primarily necrophagous flies have shown a capacity to establish cutaneous myiasis of sheep comparable with those of P. eximia. At least 6 species of Australian blowflies, particularly P. cuprina which could have had no contact with sheep before European settlement in Australia, are capable of establishing myiasis in exactly the same fashion as JR eximia. This habit, however, is only of secondary importance in their bionomics (Norris 1990). Carrion in nature invariably is loaded with far greater numbers of fly eggs than the maximum number of larvae that can mature on it (Holdaway 1930, Wardle 1930, Salt 1932, Fuller 1934, Greenberg and Szyska 1984, Baumgartner and Greenberg 1985). Great emphasis has been placed on the importance of intra-and interspecific competition in limiting blow fly production (Fuller 1934, Cragg 1955). In Brazil there are reports of competitive displacement of P. eximia by exotic species, belonging to the genus Chnjsomya (Rob. Desvoidy), introduced into the country in the late 1970s (Guimaraes et al. 1979). This fact is reinforced by data obtained in some Brazilian states, such as Goias (Ferreira 1983), Minas Gerais (Madeira et al. 1982), Rio de Janeiro (D'Almeida and Lopes 1983), and Amazonia (Paraluppi 1992). This competition could be the basis for the exploitation of new substrate as a breeding site. These reports indicate the importance of analyzing the species that cause primary myiasis particularly in domestic animals.

The mtDNA variation found in our limited sampling of P. eximia populations could prove very useful as molecular genetic markers in a species where other genetic markers have been lacking. It also provides a foundation for additional evolutionary and genetic studies of the distribution of maternal lines in this widely dispersed species.
