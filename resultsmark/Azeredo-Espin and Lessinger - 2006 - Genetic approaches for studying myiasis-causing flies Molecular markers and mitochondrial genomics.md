Myiasis-causing flies'' is a generic term that includes species from numerous dipteran families, mainly Calliphoridae and Oestridae, of which blowflies, screwworm flies and botflies are among the most important. This group of flies is characterized by the ability of their larvae to develop in animal flesh. When the host is a live vertebrate, such parasitism by dipterous larvae is known as primary myiasis. Myiasiscausing flies can be classified as saprophagous (free-living species), facultative or obligate parasites. Many of these flies are of great medical and veterinary importance in Brazil because of their role as key livestock insect-pests and vectors of pathogens, in addition to being considered important legal evidence in forensic entomology. The characterization of myiasis-causing flies using molecular markers to study mtDNA (by RFLP) and nuclear DNA (by RAPD and microsatellite) has been used to identify the evolutionary mechanisms responsible for specific patterns of genetic variability. These approaches have been successfully used to analyze the population structures of the New World screwworm fly Cochliomyia hominivorax and the botfly Dermatobia hominis. In this review, various aspects of the organization, evolution and potential applications of the mitochondrial genome of myiasis-causing flies in Brazil, and the analysis of nuclear markers in genetic studies of populations, are discussed.

### Introduction
### Myiasis
Myiasis (Greek myia = fly) is the infestation of the living tissues of animals with dipterous larvae (Zumpt, 1965). The importance of this definition lies in the fact that the larvae must complete part of their normal development in or on the vertebrate body.

In the family Calliphoridae (blowflies), at least 80 species have been recorded as agents of myiasis (Zumpt, 1965). These species can generally be divided into three functional groups based on their larval feeding habits: (1) saprophages, which normally live in decaying organic matter and animal carcasses and cannot initiate a myiasis but may secondarily invade existing infestations, (2) facultative ectoparasites, which normally adopt an ectoparasitic habit and are capable of initiating myiases, but occasionally live as facultative saprophages, and (3) primary, obligate parasites that feed only on tissues of living vertebrates, usually mammals and birds (Hall and Wall, 1995).

This functional division may reflect the evolution of the parasitic habit in calliphorid ectoparasites (Zumpt, 1965; see review by Hall and Wall, 1995). Generalist, free-living saprophagous feeders, which may occasionally act as agents of myiasis in wounded, dying or otherwise clinically predisposed animals, may have formed the ancestral origins of the parasitic habit. These then gave rise to facultative ectoparasites that were attracted to skin soiled by faeces, bacterial infections and suppurating wounds, and acted as agents of primary myiasis rather than saprophages. From this intermediate stage, obligate parasitism developed (Zumpt, 1965;Erzinc¸lioglu, 1989). In support of this general view is the finding that each genus contains species that display a range of stages in their dependency on ectoparasitism. For example, the genus Chrysomya contains the obligate ectoparasite Chrysomya bezziana (Vill.) and the secondary facultative ectoparasites Chrysomya rufifacies (Macq), Chrysomya megacephala (Fabricius), Chrysomya albiceps (Wied.) and Chrysomya putoria (Wiedemann). The genus Cochliomyia contains the obligate ectoparasite Cochliomyia hominivorax (Coquerel) and the secondary facultative ectoparasite Cochliomyia macellaria (Fabricius). The primary screwworm fly C. hominivorax, the New World screwworm (NWS), is one of the most important parasitic insect pests that causes invasive myiasis in warm-blooded vertebrates and is responsible for important economic losses to livestock rearing throughout the Neotropics. C. macellaria is a secondary agent of myiasis.

The family Oestridae (botflies) contains 28 genera and about 151 species of myiasis-producing larvae that are all obligate parasites. These flies are still classified morphologically based on the description of Zumpt (1965), and are grouped into four subfamilies: Oestrinae, Gasterophilinae, Hypodermatinae and Cuterebrinae (Hall and Wall, 1995;Otranto et al., 2000;Pape, 2001). These subfamilies have an enormous diversity of lifestyles, geographical distribution and parasite behavior within the host. Botflies are harbored and feed for several weeks or months in the host's nasopharyngeal tract (e.g., Oestrus ovis), gut (e.g., Gasterophilus ssp.), internal organs and subcutaneous tissues (e.g., Hypoderma bovis and Hypoderma lineatum), and dermal or furuncular (Dermatobia and Cuterebra) (Hall and Wall, 1995;Pape, 2001). The subfamily Cuterebrinae is restricted to the New World (Catts, 1982;Boyes, 1961;Guimara˜es & Papavero, 1999) and parasitizes rodents, lagomorphs and primates (Guimara˜es, 1967). The most important cuterebrid is Dermatobia hominis (to´rsalo, berne, human botfly), a Central and South American species, the larvae of which create boil-like swellings where they enter the skin (Lane et al., 1987). The larvae of D. hominis penetrate the host's skin without a need for lesions and produce a furuncular myiasis; these flies develop in the subcutaneous tissue of the host during most of their lifetime (reviewed in 1983, Hall andWall, 1995;Guimaraˆes and Papavero, 1999). In economic terms, D. hominis is most important as a pest of cattle, but also parasitizes man, dogs and a variety of other domestic and wild animals and birds.

D. hominis is distinguished from other myiasiscausing flies by its use of an intermediate vector for dispersal. Adult females oviposit on the abdomen of carriers, usually zoophilic flies or mosquitoes, during flight. When the carrier settles on a warmblooded animal, the eggs hatch in response to heat and release the larvae that rapidly penetrate the skin (Guimara˜es et al., 1983;Hall and Wall, 1995).

Botflies have received considerable attention probably ever since the domestication of hoofed animals that brought these flies into close contact with humans (Papavero, 1977). Botflies continue to receive substantial attention, especially from applied entomologists, because of the significant veterinary and medical importance of several species, particularly D. hominis.

### Calliphoridae in the Neotropical region
The pioneer medical descriptions of myiasis in Latin America were written by Lesbini, Weyenbergh and Conil (1878) in Argentina, and Ma-galha˜es (1895) and Bleyer (1905) in Brazil. A simplified list of the principal dipterans capable of causing myiasis followed by a brief presentation of the biology, lesions inflicted, and methods of treatment and control of myiases of economic importance were presented by Toure (1994). In addition to classic works in which myiasis is considered (James, 1947;Zumpt, 1965), more recent reviews of the worldwide literature have been provided by Hall and Wall (1995) and Guimara˜es and Papavero (1999).

The Neotropical Calliphoridae are grouped into four subfamilies: Mesembrilinae, Chrysomyinae, Calliphorinae and Toxotarsine, with approximadely 27 genera and 125 species (James, 1970). Species implicated as agents of myiasis in the Neotropical region occur in the genera Cochliomyia (Tonwsend), Compsomyiosps (Townsend), Lucilia (Robineau-Desvoidy), Calliphora (Robineau-Desvoidy) and Chrysomya (Robineau-Desvoidy).

Only C. hominivorax (Coquerel) causes obligatory myiasis whereas all the other species of the genera mentioned above are either secondary invaders of wounds or cause facultative myiasis of various types (Guimara˜es and Papavero, 1999).

For many years, our laboratory has been studying the genetic variability and evolution of myiasis-causing flies of medical and veterinary importance, with emphasis on six calliphorid species; C. hominivorax (NSW fly), C. macellaria, Chrysomya chloropyga ( = C. putoria), C. megacephala, C. albiceps, Lucilia eximia, and one oestrid species D. hominis (botfly).

Until recently, Chrysomya flies were restricted to the Old World tropics, where they are among the most abundant and economically important blowflies. The involuntary introduction of four species of this genus into the Western Hemisphere resulted in their successful colonization (Guimara˜es et al., 1978(Guimara˜es et al., , 1979)). C. megacephala (Fabricius), C. albiceps (Wiedemann) and C. chloropyga (=C. putoria) (Wiedemann) were introduced at two locations in southern Brazil, around 1975. The period 1975-1976 coincided with a great exodus of Angolan refugees to Brazil, some of whom traveled by ship accompanied by their domestic animals (Guimara˜es et al., 1978(Guimara˜es et al., , 1979;;Laurence, 1981Laurence, , 1986)). Since then, these flies have spread rapidly across South America (Mariluis, 1981a(Mariluis, , 1981b;;Baumgartner and Greenberg, 1984;Baumgartner, 1988;Wells, 1991). A fourth species, C. rufifacies (Macquart), was collected in Costa Rica in 1978 (Jiro´n, 1979). These invader species have displaced native species through competition for substrate and ecological niches (Laurence, 1988).

Chrysomya species are of considerable medical and sanitary importance because they occur in garbage, faeces and carcasses, and may act as a agents of myiasis in man and animals (Baumgatner and Greenberg, 1984). Together with other members of the Calliphoridae, these flies are of potential forensic importance because they feed and breed on exposed corpses (Salviano et al., 1996 ;Souza and Linhares, 1997;Carvalho et al., 2004).

C. chloropyga (=C. putoria) is a tropical and subtropical blowfly native to Africa. The reproduction, population dynamics, genetic variability and behavior of the introduced forms have been extensively studied (Azeredo-Espin & Pavan, 1983;Greenberg & Szyska, 1984;Avancini & Prado, 1986;Azeredo-Espin, 1987;Avancini & Linhares, 1988;Godoy, Von Zuben & Ribeiro, 1993;Valle & Azeredo-Espin, 1995). More recently, Junqueira (2002) and Junqueira, Lessinger and Azeredo-Espin (2002) analyzed the usefulness of the control region (CR) of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) as a molecular marker in a comparative temporal and geographical analysis of museum-preserved and . fresh samples of Brazilian C. putoria. The low genetic variability detected in the CR (domain A) of C. putoria could be related to the bottleneck that occurred during the recent introduction of this blowfly species in Brazil (Junqueira, 2002). C. megacephala, commonly known as the Oriental latrine-fly, is a native of the Oriental and Australasian regions, and has been found on the west coast of Africa, near Cape Town, and in Nigeria, as well as in South and Central America; this species has reached North America and has become established in the Los Angeles area (Laurence, 1981;Greenberg, 1988;Olsen & Sidebottom, 1990;Wells, 1991;Guimara˜es & Papavero, 1999). C. rufifacies (Maquart), a native of the Australasian and Pacific areas (James, 1947), was recorded from Costa Rica in 1978 as larvae recovered human cadavers (Jiro´n, 1979) and has since been recorded in Mexico and the United States (Baumgartner, 1986(Baumgartner, , 1988;;Wells, 1991;Gimara˜es & Papavero, 1999).

C. albiceps has been recorded in Africa, southern Europe, Arabia, India and, more recently, Central and South America. The larvae of this species and C. rufifacies are commonly known as ''hairy maggots'' because of the fleshy projections on their bodies. This species has been associated with myiasis in Africa (Zumpt, 1965). The taxonomic situation of C. albiceps is uncertain since it has long been thought to be indistinghishable from C. rufifacies (Richard and Gerrish, 1983). However, Tantawi and Greenberg (1993) and Erzinc¸lioglu (1987) found some morphological characteristics that allowed the differentiation of both species in the larval stage. In the Neotropical region, particularly Brazil, C albiceps is the only Chrysomya species that causes myiasis in calves in Minas Gerais (Leite et al., 1983).

More recently, an analysis based on mtDNA, including the genes for cytochrome oxidase subunits I and II, was used to infer the phylogeny and genetic divergence of C. albiceps and C. rufifacies collected from widely separated localities in the New World (Wells and Sperling, 1999). These results distinguished both species using mtDNA and indicated that the two species were monophyletic lineages that had been separated for >1 million years.

L. eximia (=Phaenicia eximia) (Robineau-Desvoidy) is a Nearctic and Neotropical species frequently found in rural, urban, and forest habitats in Brazil, Peru, and Argentina (Ferreira, 1978;Linhares, 1981;Madeira, Dias & Mascaranhas, 1982;Baumgartner & Greenberg, 1985;Mariluis et al., 1990) and in forest in the United States (Goddard & Lago, 1985). Adults of L. eximia are attracted to a wide variety of substrates for food and reproduction, including garbage, carcasses and human faeces (Mendes & Linhares, 1993). Investigations of natural populations of L. eximia have been limited to analyzing the population dynamics and behavior (Linhares, 1981;Madeira et al., 1982;Ferreira, 1983;Madeira, 1985;Wiegand et al., 1991;Paraluppi, 1992;Mendes and Linhares, 1993). These studies have shown that L. eximia is primarily necrophagous and feeds on carrion. However in Brazil, a case of primary myiasis in a young, healthy domestic dog was found to be infested with several eggs and first instar larvae of L. eximia (Azeredo-Espin and Madeira, 1996) and another reported case of this species causing primary myiasis in a domestic cat (Madeira et al., 1989). A comparative analysis of mtDNA with 12 restriction enzymes in samples from two populations of L. eximia collected on different hosts (live dog and bovine carcass) but from the same locality revealed that four enzymes were able to differentiate the two populations (Azeredo-Espin & Madeira, 1996). Carrion generally contains a far greater number of fly eggs than the maximum number of larvae that can mature in the carcass (Holdaway, 1930;Wardle, 1930;Salt, 1932;Fuller, 1934;Greenberg and Szyska, 1984;Baumgartner & Greenberg, 1985). Considerable emphasis has been placed on the importance of intra-and interspecific competition in limiting blowfly production (Fuller, 1934;Cragg, 1955). In Brazil, there are reports of competitive displacement of L. eximia and C. macellaria by exotic species of the genus Chrysomya that were introduced into the country in the late 1970s (Guimares, Prado & Buralli, 1979). This finding is reinforced by data from some Brazilian states, such as Goias (Ferreira, 1983), Minas Gerais (Madeira, Dias and Mascaranhas, 1982), Rio de Janeiro (D'Almeida & Lopes, 1983) and Amazoˆnia (Paraluppi, 1992), This competition could be the basis for the exploitation of new substrates as breeding sites, particularly in domestic animals as observed for L. eximia (Azeredo-Espin and Madeira, 1996).

C. macellaria (Fabricius), a blowfly that feeds primarily on carrion, is a secondary agent of myiasis and a purely saprophagous species. Adult flies are attracted to a wide variety of substrates for food and reproduction, including urban garbage, human and livestock faeces, carcasses and wounds, regardless of whether or not they are already infested with dipteran larvae (Hall, 1948;Zumpt, 1965;Paraluppi, 1992). Because of its synanthropic behavior, C. macellaria has been reported to be a mechanical vector of human and animal diseases. This species is very common in the Neotropics, and ranges from southern Canada to northern Argentina (Ferreira, 1983;Baumgartner and Greenberg, 1985;Dear, 1985). In North America, C, macellaria is an important species for estimating the postmortem interval in forensic entomology (Byrd and Butler, 1996;Castner, Byrd and Butter, 1996). In Brazil C. macellaria is the most frequent and constant species found on corpses in the bloated and decaying stages, and seldom occurs in the fresh stage (Freire, 1914); However, this situation has altered since the introduction of species of Chrysomya into Brazil (Salviano et al., 1996;Guimaraes & papavero,1999;Junqueira, 2002).

C. hominivorax, the NSW fly, is one of the most important agents of traumatic myiasis in the Neotropics (Guimaraes, Papavero and Prado, 1983). This species is an obligate ectoparasite that can infest almost all warm-blooded vertebrates, particularly livestock. C. hominivorax is most notorious when affecting domestic livestock and humans. Historically, the geographic distribution of C. hominivorax in the New World extended from the southern United States to Argentina. The current distribution of this species includes the Caribbean nations of Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Haiti and Jamaica, and all South American countries except for Chile: this pest is of great economic importance to livestock production (Wyss, 2000). The impact of C. hominivorax, on wildlife and humans has never been assessed economically but is undoubtedly severe. This species has been successfully eradicated from North and Central America by the sterile insect technique (SIT) (Kinipling, 1955(Kinipling, , 1959;;Graham, 1985;Wyss and Galvin, 1996;International Atomic Energy-IAEA, 2000). The present continental distribution of this fly is entirely Neotropical. In 1988, screwworm flies from the New World were recorded for the first time in Lybia (North Africa), where they infested wounds in livestock and humans (El-Azazy, 1989;Gabaj and Beesley, 1989;FAO, 1993;Cunningham et al., 1992). This was the first known established population of this species outside the Americas. In 1992, the release of irradiated sterile screw-worm fly males (obtained by the SIT) successfully eradicated C. hominivorax from Lybia (Cunningham et al., 1992;Lindquist et al., 1992).

Because of the substantial economic losses caused by this fly, and its influence on the trade of live animals among infested and non-infested countries, an international effort has been devoted to the eradication of C. hominivorax from endemic areas of Central and South America and to prevent invasions into screwworm-free areas (IAEA, 2000). However, there have been conflicting reports and speculation about the existence of non-interbreeding populations and their possible effects on the control program (LaChance et al., 1982). The identification of such populations and an understanding of the genetic variability of target populations are necessary in order to maximize the effectiveness of the eradication programme. We have been using different molecular markers to investigate the genetic variability and population structure of C. hominivorax throughout its current geographical distribution in South America in order to address these questions.

The aim of this review is to discuss the current issues and recent molecular investigations of myiasis-causing flies of the Calliphoridae and Oestridae in Brazil and other South America countries. In the first section, the RFLP and PCR-RFLP of mtDNA, and the RAPD and microsatellites that have already been analyzed or are potentially useful as molecular markers to characterize the genetic variability and population structure and to identify blowflies, screwworm flies and botflies, are described. The second section deals with the molecular characterization of genes and mitochondrial genomes that have been analyzed so far, or are potentially useful for the molecular phylogeny and identification of myiasis-causing flies. The last section considers the perspective for these approaches and future directions in this field.

### Genetic analysis of Calliphoridae and Oestridae species: a historical review
The shift from mating compatibility studies to cytogenetic investigations in the 1960s provided researchers with the first tangible genetic markers that could be used to study the genetics of calliphorid species (Kaufman and Wasserman, 1957;LaChance, 1961).

The karyotypes of C. chloropyga, C. megacephala and C. albiceps were described by Ullerich (1958Ullerich ( , 1963Ullerich ( , 1976)), Boyes and van Brink (1965), Boyes (1967) and Boyes and Shewell (1975). Although considerable morphological variation has been found in the karyotypes of the species in this family, the chromosome number is very stable at 2n = 12, with all species having five pairs of rather large, metacentric chromosomes and one pair of smaller, heteromorphic sex chromosomes (XX female and XY male; Stevens, 1908;Metz, 1916Metz, , 1922;;Keneuke, 1924;Boyes and Wilkes, 1953;Boyes and Van Brink, 1965;Boyes Shewell, 1975). Azeredo-Espin and Pavan (1983) described the karyotypes of strains of three species of Chrysomya (C. megacephala, C. albiceps and C. chloropyga ( = C. putoria)) that were introduced into Brazil. The results indicated that C. chloropyga and C. albiceps probably came from West Africa since the karyotypes of the Brazilian strains were similar to those described by Ullerich (1976) and Boyes and Shewell (1975), whereas C. megacephala came from Japan (Boyes and Shewell, 1975).

The advent of molecular genetics and of techniques for studying mitochondrial and nuclear genomes have facilitated the investigation of the genetics of individual specimens and of population structure. The variety and number of genetic markers available for studying calliphorid species has increased in the last decade. A number of primary and review articles have discussed the specific evolutionary, taxonomy and systematic implications of these markers, especially in blowflies (Hall and Wall, 1995;Stevens and Wall, 2001;Wells and Sperling, 2001;Otranto and Stevens, 2002).

These markers have expanded from traditional tools involving the cytological examination of mitotic and polytene chromosomes, genetic compatibility and isozyme analysis (Boyes, 1961;Boyes & VanBrink, 1965;Boyes and Shewell, 1975;Azeredo-Espin, 1982;Azeredo-Espin & Pavan, 1983;Bush & R.W. Neek., 1976 ;Whitten, 1980Whitten, , 1985Whitten, , 1986)); LaChance & Whitten, 1986;LaChance et al., 1982) to include a vast array of molecular markers. These contemporary markers range from the analysis of mtDNA by restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) (Roehrdanz, 1989;Roehrdanz and Johnson, 1988;Infante & Azeredo-Espin, 1995;Geurgas, Infante-Malachias and Azeredo-Espin, 2000), and polymerase chain reaction-restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) (Litjens, Lessinger & Azeredo-Espin, 2001) and sequencing of the mitochondrial genome of C. hominivorax (Lessinger et al., 2000), C. chloropyga (=C. putoria) (Junqueira et al., 2004) and D. hominis (Azeredo-Espin et al., accession number AY 463155) to highly polymorphic markers such as the mtDNA-Control Region (Lessinger and Azeredo-Espin, 2000;Junqueira et al., 2002), random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD), and microsatellite DNAs (Azeredo-Espin, 1993; Infante and Azeredo-Espin, 1995; Infante-Malachias, Yotoko and Azerodo-Espin, 1999;Azeredo-Espin, 2001;Skoda, Pornkulwat Foster, 2002;Torres et al., 2004).

One of the major advantages of this wide variety of genetic markers is the possibility of using a combination of them to address multifaceted questions relating to taxonomy, molecular evolution, phylogeny, population structure, and the molecular identification and characterization of myiasis-causing flies.

### C. hominivorax (NWS fly): a genetic overview
Although the concept of SIT is over 50 years old (Knipling, 1955 and1959) and the eradication program against C. hominivorax has been successful (Wyss & Galvin, 1996), there have been relatively few studies on the genetics of this species, and the results reported are sometimes conflicting. Kaufman & Wasserman (1957) were the first to describe the karyotype of C. hominivorax. Subsequently, more than a dozen distinct karyotypes were reported for this species and the possibility of ''mating types,'' particularly in tropical regions, was proposed (Makela & Richardson, 1978;Richardson, Ellison andAverhoff, 1982a, 1982b). Using the same techniques as Richardson and coworkers, some workers questioned the features used to define ''mating types'' (Gagne & Peterson, 1982). Dev, LaChance & Whitten (1985, 1986) reported a technique for studying the polytene chromosomes of C. hominivorax, but detected no chromosomal polymorphisms in populations of this species originating from Texas to Costa Rica.

Questions related to the possibility of ''mating types'' in C. hominivorax elevate the importance of population genetic research. Classic approaches of genetic analysis based on cytogenetic and morphological studies have been done in some geographic populations of screwworm flies from North America and Brazil, C. hominivorax is frequently considered to be a single, highly polymorphic species (Mclnnis, 1981;McInnis et al., 1983;LaChance et al., 1982;Azeredo-Espin, 1987). Richardson, Ellison and Averhoff, 1982b described several ''biotypes'' of screwworm fly from Mexico based on anatomical differences of the male genitalia. However, Gagne and Peterson (1982) found that variations in the genitalia were influenced by age and the frequency of mating, and concluded that these differences cannot be used to separate populations of screwworms.

More recently, RFLP of mtDNA has been used to analyze the genetic variation in natural populations of C. hominivorax from North and Central America and North Africa (Roehrdanz and Johnson, 1988;Roehrdanz, 1989;Taylor, Hammack and Roehrdanz, 1991). Thirty screwworm lines originating from Texas to Costa Rica and Jamaica were examined using 15 restriction endonucleases. Eleven enzymes revealed polymorphism and yielded 17 haplotypes in combination (Roehrdanz, 1989;Taylor, Hammack and Roehrdanz, 1991). The average sequence diversity among 27 mainland lines from North America was about 0.5%. The two Jamaican lines and one East Coast mainland line differed from the others by >2%. The high frequency of unique haplotypes found by Roehrdanz (1989) was also found in many of these same lines by other analyzes such as polytene chromosome, isozymes, and cuticular hydrocarbons (Dev, LaChance and Whitten, 1986;LaChance & Whitten, 1986;Pomonis, 1989;Krafsur & Whitten, 1993). The mtDNA haplotype described for screwworms from Lybia differed from the previously reported haplotypes from Mexico and Central America (Taylor, Hammack & Roehrdanz., 1991). This unusual haplotype suggested that the Lybian population had not originated from North or Central America but from some region of South America via infested livestock (Taylor, Hammack and Roehrdanz, 1991).

Variations in mtDNA have been used to study the differentiation of C. hominivorax in southern Brazil (Infante and Azeredo-Espin, 1995). RFLP analysis of mtDNA from 405 individuals representing four populations in the State of Sa˜o Paulo revealed 15 haplotypes after digestion of with 15 restriction endonucleases. The estimated average nucleotide divergence was 0.92%, which was within the range of the values for intraspecific differentiation in insect species. This divergence was low when compared with that observed between Mexican and two Jamaican lines (2.5%); the latter value indicated that these populations began to separate about a million years ago (Roehrdanz and Johnson, 1988). These authors suggested that because of the widespread distribution of screwworms in Central and South America, it is conceivable that Jamaica was colonized by flies from northern South America rather than from Mexico, and that the Jamaican flies may be more closely related to South American flies.

This interpretation, together with the fact that the first records of myiasis in South America and Brazil from 1580 and 1587, respectively (Souza, 1938;Lamas, 1981) were earlier than for Jamaica (Grainer, 1764), indicates that colonization occurred from the mainland (South America) to the island (Jamaica) and not vice versa (Infante & Azeredo-Espin, 1995). The cladogram of the geographical distribution of the observed haplotypes suggests that the screwworm populations probably belong to a single evolutionary lineage with populations interconnected by reduced gene flow; some subdivision of the geographic populations may also be present (Infante and Azeredo-Espin, 1995).

A similar analysis has been done in populations of C. macelaria from southeastern Brazil. Based on RFLP analysis of mtDNA, Valle and Azeredo-Espin (1995) and Valle (1997) concluded that Brazilian populations of C. macellaria showed genetic discontinuity with spatial separation, a situation similar to that reported for C. hominivorax, but with less intraspecific variability.

With regard to South America, PCR-RFLP of mtDNA has been used to study the diversity and population structure of seven geographically distinct populations of C. hominivorax from Uruguay (Lyra et al., 2005). Despite the high mtDNA variability based on the mean nucleotide and haplotype diversities, the distribution of mtDNA polymorphism at a geographical level among the Uruguayan screwworm populations suggested a panmitic population in the extreme south of this species' occurrence. Based on the distribution of C. hominivorax mtDNA polymorphisms in the Neotropical region, we concluded that the populations of this fly cannot be differentiated at the limits of the species' distribution, in contrast to the variation found in southeastern Brazil based on RFLP of mtDNA which showed moderate differentiation among Brazilian populations (Infante and Azeredo-Espin, 1995).

We have used nuclear DNA markers obtained by RAPD analysis (Infante-Malachias, Yotoko & Azeredo-Espin, 1999) and microsatellite markers to obtain a better understanding of the genetic structure of C. hominivorax in South America (Torres et al., 2004). RAPD analysis of seven C. hominivorax populations, six from southeastern Brazil (Adamantina, Amparo, Botucatu, Caraguatatuba, Pirassununga & Valinhos in Sa˜o Paulo states) and one from northern Argentina (Rafaela) (Figure 1), showed that the populations were closely related, based on mean of the similarity measures. The cluster analysis of Nei's unbiased genetic distance indicated that the sampled C. hominivorax populations were not strongly differentiated. However, estimates of F¢(st) (Lynch and Milligan, 1994) indicated the presence of subdivisions among the pooled screwworm populations (F¢(st) = 0.122). Past high-migration rates and recent migration may mask these values. Such migration may have been associated with the transport of host animals through the Neotropical region that started with the introduction of cattle breeding on the continent in the 16th century and later increased as the methods of transport improved.

In general, the results of mitochondrial (Roehrdanz & Johnson, 1988;Roehrdanz, 1989;Infante & Azeredo-Espin, 1995) and RAPD analyzes indicate divergence among C. hominivorax populations. However, in contrast to DNA markers, studies using isozymes have indicated low genetic differentiation among natural populations of screwworm flies in Central America and low genetic differentiation between Mexican and Central American populations (Taylor and Peterson, 1994;Krafsur and Whitten, 1993). The discrepancy between isozyme and RAPD data suggests that isozyme analyzes may underestimate genetic diversity. As indicated in other systems (Gibbs, Prion and Weatherhead, 1994), RAPD markers may evolve at a rate similar to mtDNA.

Another explanation for the discrepancies among the protein electrophoresis, RAPD, and mitochondrial results for C. hominivorax could be related to the distinct regions of sampling. Our mtDNA and RAPD results showed that the C. hominivorax populations were heterogeneous and displayed some population structuring in South America. These results differed from reports for North America screwworm populations which concluded that the species was panmitic, i.e., extremely homogeneous. We speculate that South America is probably the center of origin of this species, which could explain the variability found here. The North American populations were possibly formed by a founder effect, which would explain the homogeneity of the population.

Although RAPD has been used to study various myiasis-causing ectoparasites (Stevens andWall, 1995, 1996b;Infante-Malachias, Yotoko and Azeredo-Espin, 1999), the often poor reproducibility of RAPD fingerprints and the variation in fingerprint patterns among specimens and laboratories have significantly limited the use of this technique (Black et al., 1992).

Most of the current research projects that based on nuclear markers use microsatellite DNA or SSR (single sequence repeats). Microsatellite sequences are the most revealing DNA markers available for inferring population-genetic structure and dynamics. A number of recent reviews have dealt with the use of microsatellites in genetic studies of populations, e.g., Estoup, Jarne and Cornuet, (2002). The popularity of microsatellite DNA markers among molecular population biologists is not surprising, considering the special features of these markers and the apparent reliability of data derived from them. These simple, repetitive sequences are widely distributed throughout eukaryote organisms, show high levels of polymorphism (high mutation rates), obey Mendelian inheritance and follow apparently simple modes of evolution. Their introduction into population-genetic studies has greatly advanced our ability to assess population-genetic structure, to test parentage and relatedness, to assess genetic diversity, and to study recent population history (Estoup, Jarne and Cornuet, 2002).

We have isolated 10 polymorphic microsatellite loci from an AC-enriched genomic library. Amplification of the reported loci in 30 C. hominivorax . specimens from locations across South America revealed an average of 6.9 alleles per locus, with expected heterozygosities ranging from 0.3831 to 0.8022 (Torres et al., 2004). In addition to characterizing the variability of the 10 loci in C. hominivorax, cross-amplifications were done to test their applicability in four other calliphorid species. The results indicated that six of the loci may also be useful for genetic studies in the secondary screwworm, C. macellaria, and in other calliphorid species, such as C. putoria, C bezziana (Old World screwworm fly -OWS) and Lucilia cuprina (Torres et al., 2004).

Further analysis of mtDNA with PCR-RFLP and with the microsatellite loci characterized for C. hominivorax (Torres et al., 2004) will facilitate investigation into the genetic variability and population structure of this species from Central and South American populations and should also allow monitoring of the expansion of this pest into screwworm-free areas. Such information will lay the groundwork for future strategies for eradicating this pest in South America.

### Genetic analysis in D. hominis (botfly)
In addition to its economic importance, there is an academic interest in the botfly D. hominis because of its peculiar biology. This species has a wide geographical range that is attributable to the lack of specificity of its larvae for host animals and the lack of selectivity of female botflies for the insects that will carry their eggs. Analysis of the genetic variability of D. hominis populations based on molecular markers is a very important aspect of genetic structure studies. Although there have been no genetic studies of natural D. hominis populations, the introduction and movement of livestock in South America have probably modified the original D. hominis population structure, as is believed to have happened to other myiasis flies (Roehrdanz & Johnson, 1988;Infante & Azeredo-Espin, 1995;Taylor, Szalauski & Peterson, 1996;Stevens & Wall, 1996a, 1996b;Otranto & Stevens, 2002).

Since livestock is currently the primary host for D. hominis, we have assessed the genetic variation and population structure of this species by analyzing restriction site variation in mtDNA using RFLP and RAPD in populations derived from cattle (Figure 1; Table 1).

RFLP analysis of mtDNA was done using 227 larvae of D. hominis collected from 14 localities in southeastern, southern and central regions of Brazil (Figure 1; Table 1). Forty-eight haplotypes with a nucleotide sequence divergence of 2.75% were found using eight informative endonucleases (Geurgas, Infante-Malachias and Azeredo-Espin, 2000). The haplotypes could be divided into three clades, with interclade variation ranging from 0.94% to 2.00%, Of the 48 mitochondrial haplotypes, 15 were shared by most populations. The other 33 haplotypes were restricted to single localities, with 25 of them appearing only once in the sample. However, the diversity index (Excoffier, Snouse & Quattro, 1992) among populations was (/st = 0.07, which indicates low genetic differentiation among the populations analyzed. Analysis of molecular variation (AMOVA; Excoffier, Snouse and Quattro, 1992) showed that the high level of mtDNA variation in D. hominis was attributed to differences within populations (95.4%) and only 4.6% was attributed to differences among populations. The estimated Fst of 0.007 indicated a lack of genetic structure. The shared presence of rare haplotypes strongly suggested that there had been a recent gene flow involving females, probably reinforced by the movement of infected cattle in the region sampled. In addition to mitochondrial clades that were shared among locations, rare mtDNA haplotypes also commonly appeared at multiple locations. Normally, species with no genetic structure in their mtDNA show little genetic diversity, with a sequence divergence of <1% between haplotypes. This pattern very likely reflects an intense historical gene flow or rapid expansion of the population (Avise et al., 1987;Rogers and Harpending, 1992).

RAPD was used to detect genetic nuclear DNA polymorphism and gene flow among sampled individuals of D. hominis from six locations in four Brazilian states: Goia´s, Minas Gerais, Sa˜o Paulo and Parana´(Figure 1). Fifty-four decamer primers with random sequences were initially used to screen the D. hominis populations. Of these, seven primers. (OPJ-01, 06, 18, OPN-03, 05, 07 and 14, Operon Technologies, Alameda, Calif., that produced the clearest and most unambiguous bands were chosen for subsequent experiments.

The mean similarity measures obtained (S and M) showed that the populations were closely related. Although the intrapopulational similarity index of >0.7 was higher than the interpopulational index (0.6) (Tables 2 and 3), the dendrograms obtained from these parameters showed no population structure (Yotoko, 1998). The mean heterozygosity calculated with the Lynch and Milligan (1994) modifications showed that the genetic divergence in the populations was low (Table 4) and that the values were similar among populations, which also indicates a high gene flow among them. The mean F¢(st) of 0.05 indicated that these D. hominis populations were not structured.

The lack of population structure and the shared presence of rare mtDNA haplotypes, together with the similar results obtained by RAPD, suggested a recent contact among locations that had probably been facilitated by livestock movements, particularly because there was no relation between geographic distance and the similarity among populations. Contact among populations may be maintained primarily by carrier phoretic insects, followed by host transport that started when cattle were introduced into the Americas in the 16th century (Lamas, 1981) and which was later accelerated by improved methods of transporting cattle. Comparison of the results obtained by RFLP of mtDNA and RAPD suggests that in Brazil, D. hominis is a panmitic species (Yotoko, 1998;Geurgas, Infante-Malachias and Azeredo-Espin, 2000).

### Structural organization and evolution of the mitochondrial) genome in myiasis-causing flies
### Historical
In addition to investigating the genetic variability and molecular markers in myiasis-causing flies, we have been working on the structural diversity and evolution of mitochondrial genomes in calliphorids and other dipterans of medical and veterinary importance. In 1999, when we initiated these studies, the sequencing of complete mitochondrial genomes was a pioneering area of research in Brazil. The publication of the mitochondrial genomes of the NSW fly C. hominivorax (Lessinger et al., 2000) and the blowfly C. chloropyga (Junqueira et al., 2004) represented the only two complete mitochondrial genomes of calyptrates among the first 13 arthropodan mitochondrial The recent development of genomic technologies and the importance of the mitochondrial genome as an informative molecular marker for evolutionary studies have resulted in a substantial increase in the number of completely sequenced mitochondrial genomes. There are almost 700 metazoan species for which the mitochondrial genome has been sequenced so far (http://ami-ga.cbmeg.unicamp.br). Vertebrate and arthropod mitochondrial genomes predominate among these sequences, although the 86 arthropodan mitochondrial genomes described to date still represent only a very small number considering the biological diversity of this group.

The analysis of complete mitochondrial genomes in invertebrates began in 1985 with the description of the Drosophila yakuba mitochondrial genome (Clary and Wolstenholme, 1985), which provided a model for the structural organization of the arthopodan mitochondrial genome and represented a significant stimulus for further mtDNA studies. More dipteran mitochondrial genomes have since been described, including those of two Anopheline species (Beard, Ham and Collins, 1993;Mitchell Cockburn and Seawright, 1993), D. melanogaster (Lewis, Farr and Kaguni, 1995), Ceratitis capitata (Spanos et al., 2000), C. hominivorax (Lessinger et al., 2000), three other Drosophila species (Ballard, 2000a(Ballard, , 2000b)), another tephritid fly, Bactrocera oleae (Nardi et al., 2003a), the blowfly C. chloropyga (Junqueira et al., 2004), and the botfly, D. hominis (Azeredo-Espin et al., accession number AY463155). Currently, the mitochondria) genomes of 13 dipteran species have been completely (or almost completely) sequenced, and represent 28% of hexapodan and $15% of arthropodan mitochondrial genomes in the NCBI database.

### General structure and phylogenetic perspectives
The mitochondrial genomes of C. hominivorax (accession number AF260826) and C. chloropyga (accession number AF352790) are circular molecules of 16,022 and 15,837 bp, respectively. Their gene content and general organization are similar to that of the D. yakuba mitochondrial genome (Clary and Wolstenholme, 1985), except for a 123 bp repeated region in C. chloropyga, adjacent to the control region, that contains duplicated tRNA sequences. A usual gene content of 13 protein-coding genes and two rRNA genes has been found in both species. However, only C. hominivorax has the 22 tRNA genes frequently described in animal mitochondrial genomes, whereas C. chloropyga has a set of 23 tRNAs that includes an additional copy of tRNA Ile . The main non-coding region, identified as the mtDNA CR, is located between the tRNA Ile and srRNA genes in the screwworm (1175 bp) and between the two tRNA IIe copies in the blowfly (812 bp). Both CRs contain several conserved sequence blocks (CSBs) previously described as potential regulatory elements in myiasis-causing flies (Lessinger and Azeredo-Espin, 2000).

As the number of complete mitochondrial genomes for arthropods increases, so does the possibility of screening these genomes, nucleotide sequences, predicted protein sequences and gene order for useful phylogenetic information (Boore, Lavrov and Brown, 1998). Most commonly, alignments of predicted amino acid sequences from a set of homologous mitochondrial genes have been concatenated and used to infer phylogenetic relationships among arthropods (Hwang et al., 2001;Nardi et al., 2003b,c). However, any phylogenetic inference based on low numbers and poor taxa sampling of the range of possible mitochondria) genomes (Nematocera; two anophelines; Brachycera: five drosophilids, two tephritids, two calliphorids and one oestrid) may be insufficient to confirm or reject competing hypotheses. The monophyletic condition of the Calyptratae and Acalyptratae assemblages have been confirmed (Lessinger et al., 2000;Junqueira et al., 2004) and rejected (Nardi et al., 2001), depending on the methods of analysis and the subsets of mitochondria) genes used. As an alternative to phylogenetic analysis based on amino acid sequences, a recent approach using mtDNA protein-coding nucleotide data (RY-coded) to reconstruct Hexapoda phylogenetics (Delsuc, Phillips & Penny, 2003) has clustered the calyptrate and acalyptrate species as monophyletic groups.

### Control region (or A + T-rich region)
In animal mtDNA, the analysis of non-coding sequences can provide important data for screening potentially informative molecular markers for use in population genetics, phylogenetics, species identification and the analysis of molecular evolution. The sequences of insect mtDNA control regions have been used as efficient molecular markers in Drosophila phylogeography (Brehm et al., 2004), for population genetics (Schultheis, Weigt & Hendricks, 2002;Lyra et al. (2005), for resolving lepidopteran phylogeny (Vila and Bjorklund, 2004), for analyzing polymorphisms in Aedes aegypti (Duenas et al., 2002), for DNA-based taxonomical identification of screwworms (Litjens, Lessinger & Azeredo-Espin, 2001), and for efficiently retrieving genetic information from museum specimens (Junqueira, Lessinger & Azeredo-Espin, 2002).

However, the lack of structural conservation of CR sequences and their flanking genes, even among closely related species, limits the use of universal insect mtDNA primers (Simon et al., 1994) and requires special care in the interpretation of homologous sequences in comparative analyzes. Furthermore, the presence of a potential origin of replication or regulatory elements, long polynucleotide stretches (such as poly-A and poly-T arrays), fast-evolving primary sequences, and structurally instable elements, such as multiple repeats and sequences able to form secondary structures, may increase the technical and methodological difficulties related to access to mtDNA CR sequence data. Indeed, projects designed to sequence the complete mitochondrial genome of arthropods have commonly reported technical difficulties in accessing and sequencing the CR or have not sequenced this region at all (Yamauchi, Miya & Nishida, 2004). Other important questions addressing structural evolution of CR sequences have recently been examined by the analysis of duplicated control regions in ticks (Shao et al., 2005).

We have identified two distinct structural domains related to conserved (A) and variable (B) sequences in the CR of the mitochondrial genome of myiasis-causing flies (Lessinger & Azeredo-Espin, 2000;Lessinger et al., 2004). The identification of eight CSBs among several species of Calliphoridae and Oestridae indicates that these genomic systems may share a similar regulatory mechanism for controlling replication and/or transcriptional processes. Preliminary results in the characterization of Muscidae mitochondrial genomes suggest that this regulatory system may be a conserved feature among calyptrate flies (MTO, unpublished results). On the other hand, the variable B domain appears to evolve with no evident selective pressure (except towards a high AT content) and is therefore considered a hyper-variable region because of the lack of confidence in sequence alignments and the absence of structural homologies. Despite the characterization of structural and regulatory elements, the mtDNA CR of myiasis-causing flies has been used for the taxonomical identification of screwworm species using PCR-RBLP diagnostic markers (Litjens, Lessinger & Azeredo-Espin, 2002) and may be highly informative for screening the molecular identity of blowflies because of the presence of hyper-variable sequences (Lessinger et al., 2004) that could provide suitable species-specific markers for DNA-based investigations in forensic entomology.

### Duplicated genes
Based on the recent characterization of numerous mitochondrial genomes from a wider range of metazoan species, new assumptions on mitochondrial genome structural diversity and evolution have arisen, many of which resulted from the sequencing of arthropodan mitochondrial genomes (Black and Roehrdanz, 1998;Lavrov, Brown & Boore, 2000;Shao, Campbell & Barker, 2001;Shao & Barker, 2003;Thao, Baumann & Baumann, 2004;Masta & Boore, 2004;Negrisolo, Minelli & Valle, 2004;Junqueira et al., 2004). Important contributions on the characterization of new structural features can be gained by analyzing the mitochondrial genome of species from wellstudied orders, such as dipterans. We have recently identified duplicated coding sequences in blowflies (Lessinger et al., 2004). The prediction of functional duplicated genes in animal mitochondrial genomes is restricted to tRNA genes: tRNA Pro in an anphisbaenian (Macey et al., 1998), tRNA Phe in snakes (Kumazawa et al., 1998), tRNA Met in Mytilus (Beagley, Okimoto & Wolstenholme 1999) and tRNA ser in Thrips (Shao & Barker, 2003). Gene duplications are believed to represent an intermediate state of the ''duplication-random-loss model'' that operates in the mitochondrial genome (Macey, 1998). At the moment, it is still unclear whether gene conversion or selective constraints help to maintain conserved gene copies in Chrysomya mitochondrial genomes (Lessinger et al., 2004).

Another interesting approach related to the identification of duplicated sequences in the mitochondrial genomes of these species involves mapping this structural feature in a blowfly phylogenetic topology. This procedure may be a useful tool for inferring evolutionary processes related to the origin of tRNA duplication, and for predicting its distribution in the mitochondrial genomes of other closely related species. Recently, Wells and Sperling (2001) provided a blowfly (Chrysomyinae) phylogeny based on mtDNA COI/COII sequences that could be used to determine the taxononlical identity of unknown samples of forensically important species, The basal position of C albiceps and the internal positions of C. chloropyga and C. megacephala in this phylogeny (Figure 2) indicated that the duplication event may have occurred before the diversification of Chrysomya and may represent a synapomorphy of the Chrysomya assemblage. In support of the idea that this duplicated state may represent a stable feature in Chrysomya evolutionary history, we found no homologous duplicated regions in other myiasis-causing flies for which the CRs have been sequenced (Lessinger & Azeredo-Espin, 2000), including C. hominivorax, C. macellaria, L. eximia and D. hominis. The characterization of structural motifs should provide potentially informative markers for elucidating the relationships among the Calliphoridae, and should improve our understanding of the evolution of myiasis in freeliving organisms and parasitic species.

### Contributions of myiasis-causing flies to the study of mitochondrial genomes
The sequencing of mitochondrial genomes could help to improve the analysis of molecular markers in myiasis-causing flies, especially the identification of potential primer annealing sites in conserved, taxon-related sequences and the mapping of endonuclease restriction sites for the identification of potential candidate regions for PCR-RFLP. The resulting sequencing data could be useful for comparative genomics and phylogenetic analyzes. Simon et al. (1994) provided an indication of the importance of compiling a set of conserved primers for retrieving mtDNA sequences for phylogenetic studies (815 citations). Despite the usefulness of ''universal'' mtDNA primer sets in a variety of organisms, a few specific regions of the mitochondrial genome (such as the CR) failed to amplify or could not be consistently retrieved using ''universal primers.'' For these cases, the availability of complete mitochondrial genome sequences for a particular or multiple taxa could provide an important source of information for designing useful primers.

The articles describing the mitochondrial genome of C. hominivorax (Lessinger et al., 2000) and the structure and evolution of CR sequences in myiasis-causing flies (Lessinger & Azeredo-Espin, 2000) have been cited more than 20 and 14 times so far, respectively (ISI-Web of Science). This parameter may provide an objective tool for assessing the contribution of our studies on the structural analysis of mitochondrial genomes in myiasis-causing flies. A qualitative analysis of these records classified the contributions of the mitochondrial genome of C. hominivorax into the following categories: other projects involved in mitochondrial genome characterization and/or sequencing, molecular studies of myiasis-causing flies or other insect pests, and general literature on insect molecular genetics (Hoy, 2003). The CR article was cited by reports dealing with evolutionary studies, methodological descriptions, species identification, structural and molecular evolution, and complete mitochondrial genome analysis. Perspectives 1. A major environmental and economic concern is the continued development of resistance by insects to chemical insecticides. In the past 10 years, the molecular basis of insect resistance to many chemical insecticides has been elucidated. Although chemical insecticides are the only available method for controlling myiasis-causing flies in Brazil, and have been successful in controlling many myiasis-causing larvae, there are many constraints associated with the use of these drugs because of the risks to animals, e.g., alimentary toxicity, environmental pollution, and the development of parasite resistance. In the case of C. hominivorax, the inappropriate use of these chemical agents can select resistant flies. On-going research projects in our laboratory include the survey of C. hominivorax for the presence and characterization of point mutations associated with esterase-mediated resistance to organophosphate. This study should enable us to determine how many mutations there are in this gene and how often these mutations arise in natural populations of C. hominivorax. The identification of resistant individuals should allow more effective strategies for controlling this species to be implemented.

2. As part of a project to investigate the genetic variability of myiasis-causing flies, we are currently producing a microsatellite-enriched library to characterize the genetic structure of exotic and endemic blowflies, especifically Chrysomya and C. macellaria species and the botfly D. hominis.

3. Although there has been progress in the molecular genetics of myiasis-causing flies (and in Arthropods in general), successful comparative mitochondrial genomics will require: (1) the development of specific databases and bioinformatics tools, (2) the availability of technically easy methods for recovering mtDNA molecules and screening for rearrangements in gene order, (3) access to variable mtDNA sequences for comparative analyzes of closely related species, (4) an increase in the number of mitochondrial genomes sequenced for species from diverse taxonomical groups, including dipterans, and (5) simultaneous initiatives and collaborations in large-scale mitochondrial genome sequencing projects.

4. On-going research projects in our laboratory that may contribute to the development of comparative mitochondrial genonics in insects include the design of a database for arthropodan mitochondrial genomes (http://amiga.cbmeg.unicamp. br), the optimization of long-PCR amplification strategies for dipteran mitochondrial genomes (Barau et al., 2005), the design of taxon-specific conserved primers to allow access to variable sequences of these genomes, such as the control region, and the characterization of other strategically informative dipteran mitochondrial genomes.
