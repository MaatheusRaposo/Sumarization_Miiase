Studies of carrion fauna have increased in Brazil and have contributed to the knowledge of this fauna in the national territory. Brazil has continental dimensions and presents various biomes in its territorial area. Most of the carrion fauna have seldom been studied, and this is especially true for the Savanna or "Cerrado." The present research examined the fauna visiting carrion in two environments and two seasons in a rural area situated in a region of Cerrado in southeastern Brazil. The two environments studied were a pasture and a fragment of semi-deciduous forest. Samples were collected during the dry and humid seasons of the year. The study identified the diversity and relative abundance of species attracted to pig carcasses (Sus scrofa L.) exposed in each environment and season. Eight pig carcasses (10 ± 1 kg) were placed in traps, and adult insects attracted to the traps were collected during the decay of the carcasses. A total of 92,489 insects were collected during the experiments. The Diptera was the most frequent order (92.2%) represented by 27 families and at least 124 species. This was followed by Coleoptera (4.4%) represented by 14 families and at least 65 species. Sarcophagidae had the greatest diversity of species, followed by Muscidae and Calliphoridae. Several species were collected only during a specific period of the year or in a single environment. The results are important from an ecological point of view and for Medico-Legal Forensic Entomology.

### Introduction
Exposed carrion in outdoor environments attracts a great diversity of necrophagous insects that use the organic matter as a source of food for their immature and/or adult stages. Omnivorous insects feed on the remains of carcasses and on the associated arthropods, which may be also hosts to parasitic insects. Predators also visit the carcasses to interact with other species. Additionally, the adventives species use the carrion as an extension of their habitat? (Smith 1986, Hanski 1987, Campobasso et al 2001).

Coleoptera and Diptera are the main groups of insects associated with carrion (Hanski 1987, Catts & Goff 1992, Goff 2000, Campobasso et al 2001, Carvalho et al 2004). Members of other orders, such as Hymenoptera, Homoptera, Isoptera, and Dermaptera, also use this substrate (Catts & Goff 1992, Rosa et al 2011, Moretti et al 2013). The richness of the carrion fauna and the time required for its decomposition vary according to several factors including geographic region and climatic conditions (Campobasso et al 2001, Anderson 2010).

Studies of carrion fauna have increased in the last 20 years in Brazil and have presented a rich fauna associated with carrion in natural and anthropic environments. These studies have increased knowledge on the subject and also on the biodiversity associated with carrion in this country (Barros et al 2008, Pujol-Luz et al 2008, Biavati et al 2010, Mise et al 2010, Rosa et al 2011, Faria et al 2013).

The Brazilian Savanna or "Cerrado" is the second largest biome in this country and considered one of the 25 areas of most interest for research in the world. It is the largest and richest tropical Savanna. Presently, this region is composed of natural and anthropic areas used for agro-pastoral activities. The extensive biodiversity of the area, however, has been little studied (Ratter et al 1997, Myers et al 2000, Klink & Machado 2005). This biome is composed of various profiles of vegetation that include "campo limpo" (clean fields), "campo sujo" (dirty fields), "Cerrado stricto sensu," and "Mata mesofítica" (mesophitic forest). The physiognomy of this last profile refers to erect and mesophytic trees and is generally close to rivers and streams in the Cerrado region (Eiten 1972).

Studies of carrion fauna in the Cerrado have been initiated only in the last few years in this country (Pujol-Luz et al 2008, Rosa et al 2011, Faria et al 2013, Mello-Patiu et al 2014). Moreover, knowledge of carrion fauna in any given region requires studies of the prevailing environment. Studies developed in this region have reported dipteran species breeding in pig carrion, discovered a rich carrion fauna, and presented new observations of several species of Sarcophagidae found in this biome (Rosa et al 2009, Rosa et al 2011, Faria et al 2013, Mello-Patiu et al 2014).

This study was conducted in a rural area where the Cerrado is the predominant biome and the agro-pastoral activities are of great economic importance. To complement previous studies (Rosa et al 2009, Rosa et al 2011, Faria et al 2013), data, both in the pasture and in a fragment of semideciduous forest, were collected. The experiments were conducted to study the fauna, attracted to the pig carrion, and its breeding in the two areas and two marked seasons. Data on the breeding insects have been published separately in Faria et al (2013). The data presented in Faria et al (2013) were restricted to 17 species of Diptera and two species of Coleoptera that developed in the carcasses in different seasons, environments, and stages of decomposition. The present study focused on the diversity and abundance of insects attracted to pig carrion in the two environments and seasons of the rural area of Uberlândia, State of Minas Gerais.

### Material and Methods
### Study area
The study was conducted at an Experimental Farm belonging to the Universidade Federal de Uberlândia (UFU), located 2 km from the urban region of the city of Uberlândia (18°57′ S and 48°12′ W), in the State of Minas Gerais, southeastern Brazil. The first site was a pasture with grass (Brachiaria brizantha, Grisebach) and direct sunlight on the vegetation at ground level. The second was a fragment of semi-deciduous forest with predominant woody vegetation composed of heterogeneous natural formations and gradual transition to a gallery forest with abrupt transitions to borders composed of artificial grassland (Haridasan & Araújo 2005

### The experiments
The experiments were conducted during the two seasons: the first was in the wet summer season, from February to March, and the second was performed during the dry, winter season, from July to September, 2010. Four domestic pig carcasses (Sus scrofa) were used in each of these two seasons: two in the pasture and two in the forest, totaling four carcasses exposed in each area and eight pig carcasses during the total experiment. Carcasses weighing 10 ± 1 kg were placed in cages of 40 × 60 × 40 cm constructed using mesh of 15 × 2 cm.

A pyramidal trap with a metal frame covered by thin cotton gauze was placed above the cages to collect adult winged insects. Removable trays, 2 cm deep, containing sawdust were placed under the cages to retain ambulatory and immature insects (data from the breeding insects have been published separately (Faria et al 2013). Adult insects were captured in the traps and collected daily in the sawdust. They were sacrificed using ethyl ether and maintained in 70% ethanol for later identification.

The temperature and relative humidity of the air were monitored by thermo-hygrometers placed close to the carcasses in each area, during the study. Rainfall data were obtained from the weather station at UFU, located 6 km from the study areas. Data on the temperature, humidity, and rainfall were registered daily throughout the decomposition process. More details of the procedures adopted in the research are described in Rosa et al (2011) and Faria et al (2013). The insect identifications were made with the aid of taxonomists (third and fourth authors) and using the following keys: McAlpine et al (1981), Almeida & Mise (2009), Pinto e Vairo et al (2011), andKosmann et al (2013).

### Statistical and faunal analyses
The frequencies of the main insect groups including Calliphoridae, Sarcophagidae, Muscidae, and Fanniidae (Diptera) as well as Staphylinidae and Histeridae (Coleoptera) in each season and area studied were compared using ANOVA. Thereafter, the Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated to test for relationships between the duration of the stages of decomposition and the frequencies of the previously mentioned families. Their frequencies were transformed into log 10 + 1 before being subjected to comparisons. The Shannon-Wiener (H′) and equitability (J) indices were used to determine the dimension of diversity and the degree of uniformity of the distribution of species in the environments and seasons studied (Odum 1988). The daily temperatures and relative humidity, as well as the indices of diversity obtained in each environment and season, were compared using the t test (Zar 1999). Statistical analyses were performed using the 5% level of significance.

### Results and Discussion
Decomposition of the pig carcasses lasted 33 days in the two environments studied during the humid season. In the dry season, this process lasted 65 days in the pasture and 51 days in the forest. The two periods presented marked differences between their climatic conditions, with the largest discrepancies being observed in mean relative humidity and in the total rainfall. The wet season was warmer and significantly more humid than the dry season (t = 18.387; P < 0.01), with average daily temperatures of 25.7 ± 2°C, relative humidity of 67.6 ± 9.3%, and total precipitation of 172.4 mm 3 . The dry season presented average temperatures of 21.8 ± 3.2°C and relative humidity of 30.3 ± 7.9% but without pluvial precipitation records ( conducted presents Aw climate, with two defined seasons: dry winter and wet summer. The average annual rainfall and the daily temperatures are around 1500 mm and 19 to 27°C, respectively (Silva & Assunção 2004). Although its absence of rainfall is not common for more than 2 months in the dry season in this area, the observed physical conditions corroborate those expected for the respective seasons of the study (Alvarez et al 2014). Different physical conditions were also observed between the pasture and forest environments. In general, the pasture was dryer (t = 11.540; P < 0.01) and hotter (t = 9.235; P < 0.01) than the forest (Fig 2). The carcasses attracted a total of 92,489 insects belonging to seven orders: Diptera (92.2%), Coleoptera (4.4%), Hemiptera (1.9%), Lepidoptera (0.9%), Hymenoptera (.5%), Odonata (0.002%), and Orthoptera (0.002%) (Table 1). Diptera were numerous in all environments and seasons with 85,302 of the individuals belonging to 27 families and at least 124 species. Sarcophagidae presented was the most abundant (48.3%) among the dipterans, followed by Ulidiidae (18.1%) and Muscidae (11.3%). The other Diptera that were considerable abundant were Calliphoridae (9.3%), Tachinidae (4.9%) and Fanniidae (2.3%) (Table 2). A total of 17 species of Diptera and two Coleoptera reproduced in the carcasses. Data on the breeding fauna including abundance and diversity, seasons, and areas where species were reared, and description and duration of the decay stages of the carcasses have been published separately in Faria et al (2013).

In general, the dry season had a greater abundance of insects (63.2%). Low humidity, prevalent during the dry season, accelerated the dehydration of the carcasses and would have restricted their colonization by the breeding fauna, retarding the decomposition process (Faria et al 2013). The correlation coefficient between duration of the decomposition and frequencies of insects was positive and significant (r = 0.49; P < 0.01). For this reason, the higher abundance of insects attracted in the dry season was, at least in part, due to the longer duration of the decomposition process, attracting the insects for a longer time. This event has been observed in other studies in Brazil (Souza & Linhares 1997, Carvalho & Linhares 2001, Rosa et al 2011).

Daily collection of insects during the longer period of the dry season resulted in a greater number of insects collected during this period. These results were influenced by the frequency obtained for Sarcophagidae, the most abundant family. Sarcophagidae represented, respectively, 48 and 44% of all dipterans and insects collected. This family presented a greater abundance in this period of the year (F = 7.274, P = 0.007) and would have been influenced significantly by this factor (Table 1).

The humid period presented greater diversity and uniformity of indices for the main families: Sarcophagidae (H′ = 1.979; J = 0.519 versus H′ = 1.3166; J = 0.3383) (t = 36.829; P < 0.01), Muscidae (H′ = 2.029; J = 0.689 versus H′ = 1.4099; J = 0.4878) (t = 24.478; P < 0.01), and Calliphoridae (H′ = 0.550; J = 0.250 versus H′ = 0.3244; J = 0.1667) (t = 10.935; P < 0.01). Only Fanniidae presented higher faunal indices in the dry period (H′ = 1.254; J = 0.644 versus H′ = 0.5574; J = 0.5077) (t = 23.779; P < 0.01). The pasture showed higher faunal indices for Muscidae (H′ = 1.510; J = 0.522 versus H′ = 1.384; J = 0.4545) (t = 4.1709; P < 0.01), while Sarcophagidae presented higher indices in the forest (H′ = 1.843; J = 0.484 versus H′ = 1.200; H = 0.3118) (t = 23.106; P < 0.01). The reduced richness of species observed in Sarcophagidae in the pasture was probably due to the lower phenotypic plasticity of part of its members to adapt to the physical and biological conditions of this environment. The environmental homogeneity of the pasture probably restricted the diversity of species of this group (Diniz et al 2010, Newbold et al 2015). If it is the case, the results strengthen the relevance of the remaining natural (forest) environment in the rural area to preserve the biodiversity of necrophagous insects in this region (Newbold et al 2015).

Studies by Barros et al (2008) (Souza & Paseto 2015) with specimens of this species collected in this same study area. Oxysarcodexia was the most diverse genus with at least 17 species and occurred in all stages of decomposition of the carcasses. This genus is characteristically Neotropical and prefers to breed in feces (Lopes 1973). The reasons that its members are attracted to carrion include the search for a source of nourishment for development of female ovocites (Mendes & Linhares 1993, Archer & Elgar 2003) and for mates. Oxysarcodexia thornax (Walker) was the most abundant and was collected in all seasons, environments, and stages of carcass decomposition. Oxysarcodexia diana (Lopes), Tricharaea (Sarcophagula) occidua (Fabricius), Sarcodexia lambens (Wiedemann), Peckia (Euboettcheria) collusor (Curran & Walley), and Ravinia belforti (Prado & Fonseca) were also quite abundant in the two periods of the year (Table 2).

The Muscidae family was represented mainly by Biopyrellia bipuncta (Wiedeman), Atherigona orientalis (Schiner), and Ophyra aenescens (Wiedemann), collected in all stages of decomposition. Stomoxys calcitrans (Linnaeus) occurred only in the last two stages of decomposition. Among the Calliphoridae, Chrysomya albiceps (Wiedemann) was the most abundant species. Chrysomya have been found to present a high degree of adaptability to conditions created by man (Nuorteva 1963). Members of this genus have caused myiases and transmitted pathogens to human and other animals in various parts of the world (Zumpt 1965, Greenberg 1971, Guimarães & Papavero 1999). Chrysomya albiceps is also considered to be of great forensic importance in Brazil as well as in several other countries (Carvalho et al 2000, Grassberger et al 2003, Vanin et al 2009, Kosmann et al 2011). This species has been the most abundant among the blowflies and has been collected in all stages of decomposition of carcasses (Carvalho & Linhares 2001, Souza et al 2008). It was also the most abundant species in the forest in the present study and has also been the dominant species in carrion exposed in other natural areas of Cerrado in Brazil (Biavati et al 2010, Rosa et al 2011, Faria et al 2013). This finding reinforces the assumption that C. albiceps is highly adapted to this region, despite its recent introduction into the Americas (Guimarães et al 1979, Linhares 1981, Baumgartner & Greenberg 1984). Cochliomyia macellaria (Fabricius) and Lucilia eximia (Wiedemann) were both present in the two experiments and at all stages of decomposition of the carcasses. On the other hand, Hemilucilia segmentaria (Fabricius) was encountered only in the first two stages of decomposition of the carcasses. The presence of Cochliomyia hominivorax (Coquerel) in the first stage of decomposition must also be noted. This species is commonly attracted to living tissues, and its larvae cause obligatory myiasis in humans and other animals of the New World (Guimarães & Papavero 1999).

The Coleoptera order was the second most abundant, represented mainly by Dermestidae (44.7%), Histeridae (19.1%), Cleridae (14.1%), Staphylinidae (14.0%), Chrysomelidae (3.7%), and Scarabaeidae (2.1%). While Staphylinidae and Histeridae were represented by several species, Dermestidae, Cleridae, and Silphidae were represented by only one species each. Staphylinidae and Histeridae occurred in greater abundance during the wet season. In the dry season, Dermestidae and Cleridae were collected in great abundance. Dermestes maculatus (De Geer) was the most abundant beetle species (44.7%) and was collected mainly in the pasture and in the dry season. The presence of large numbers of individuals of this species in the dry season was probably due to the predominance of low humidity during this period since it prefers dry and warm environments (Mise et al 2007). Dermestes maculatus has also been associated with the skeletization of human corpses, a fact that demonstrates its importance for forensic entomology (Schroeder et al 2002). Hister curvatus (Erichson) was the main representative of Histeridae during the wet period and Philonthus spp. (Staphylinidae) predominated in the pasture (62.4%), also in the wet season (48.6%) (Table 2). These two families have been the most diverse among the beetles collected in carrion in other states of this country including São Paulo, Pernambuco, Paraná, and Amazonas (Souza & Linhares 1997, Ganho & Marinoni 2003, Cruz & Vasconcelos 2006, Mise et al 2010). Hymenoptera presented smaller overall abundance as compared to Coleoptera and Diptera. However, the samples collected belonged to a great diversity of species from ten families. Formicidae stood out with 29.7% of the total sample of the order. This was followed by Chrysididae (20.9%), Apidae (18.2%), and Vespidae (17.2%). Camponotus crassus (Mayr) (For micida e) was the most abundant species representing 58.6% of the family. This species was restricted to the dry season and was more abundant in the forest. It was also noted that members of this species were observed causing skin lesions on the carcasses at the beginning of the decomposition process. Some Hymenoptera feed on body fluids as an additional source of food but are not essentially scavengers (Campobasso et al 2001). Ants, in turn, can feed on dipterous larvae and pupae (Moretti et al 2013, Paula et al 2016). Lesions caused by C. crassus on the carcasses are of special interest to forensic entomology because when ants act as scavengers, they can mutilate and cause other lesions leading to misinterpretations during forensic investigation (Moretti et al 2007, Campobasso et al 2009).

The results presented here are important from an ecological point of view as well as for Medico-Legal Forensic Entomology. They present a great diversity of insects associated with pig carrion in an area belonging to a biome whose biodiversity is little known. The species richness observed in the forest and pasture indicated the possible occurrence of some species only in the natural (forest) environment in this region. Additional studies need to be conducted to confirm the specificity of environmental and seasonal priorities of several species.
