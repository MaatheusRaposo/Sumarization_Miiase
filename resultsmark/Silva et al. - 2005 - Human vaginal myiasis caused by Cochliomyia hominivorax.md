Myiasis is a parasitic infestation that results from an infestation of the tissues or organs of live humans or vertebrate animals by the larvae of dipterous flies [1][2][3][4]. Larvae or maggots are capable of infesting and inducing myiasis in natural cavities such as the nasal fossae or eyes. They may penetrate further, causing urinary tract or gastrointestinal myiasis [1]; however vaginal myiasis is extremely rare in humans [3,4].

An obese, diabetic, hypertensive, 87-year-old Brazilian woman, gravida 13, para 13, low socioeconomic status, living in a rural area, was hospitalized with a history of pain and intense vaginal pruritus for one week prior to admission. Gynecological examination revealed cystocele, retrocele,

precarious conditions of vulvovaginal hygiene and the presence of a great number of larvae in the introitus and in the vaginal cavity (Fig. 1). Treatment consisted of removing the larvae, antisepsis of the vulvar region and daily washing of the vaginal canal

The patient was discharged four days after she was hospitalized with instructions regarding personal hygiene and the use of underwear. The larvae were identified as Cochliomyia hominivorax. The patient was reexamined two weeks after removal of the larvae and showed no sign of infestation.

Myiasis remains an important problem in medical and veterinary science. It is an infestation of live human and vertebrate animals by dipterous larvae that, at least for a period of time, feed on a host's dead or living tissue, body fluids or on ingested food in order to complete partial or total development of their evolutive cycle [2].

Predisposing factors considered important are the presence of pre-existing, suppurating lesions that attract flies and stimulate the female insects to deposit eggs or larvae, acquired immunodeficiency, comatose conditions, habits among the population of sitting on the ground as, for example, during certain religious rites, poor personal hygiene, principally among some residents of rural areas and certain climactic conditions that favor the flies [2,4].

Genital myiasis is extremely rare [2][3][4] and the woman in our report presented no signs of vulval or vaginal sores and it would appear that the larvae were deposited in the vagina by insects attracted by the patient's poor vulvovaginal hygiene, together with her habit of wearing no undergarments. Following diagnosis, treatment is simple and effective, consisting of removal of the larvae and washing of the vulva and vagina with an antiseptic solution.
