A 56-year-old Brazilian woman with psychiatric disturbance was found unconscious in a forested rural area in a state of precarious general hygiene with multiple scratches all over her body and a fracture of her left collarbone. Gynaecological examination revealed areas of vulvar-perineal necrosis secondary to infestation by fly larvae (Fig. 1). Speculum examination revealed an epithelised cervix and a Papanicolaou smear showed no signs of malignancy. Due to the presence of areas of necrosis in the vulvar and perineal areas, the patient was submitted to surgical debridement of the region during which a great quantity of larvae was removed and the area was cleaned with antiseptic solution. During surgical debridement, we observed that the anal canal and sphincter musculature were also affected. A sigmoid loop colostomy was carried out. The patient remained in the hospital for 10 days during which she was given antibiotics, analgesics and anti-inflammatory medication, and was submitted to daily local asepsis of the area with an antiseptic solution. Larvae had to be removed up to the fourth day of hospitalisation. The fracture was treated expectantly and routine laboratory tests presented no abnormal results. The larvae were identified as Cochliomyia hominivorax. The woman's lesions were completely healed after two months and the colostomy was closed after one year.

### Discussion
Myiasis is a zoonosis resulting from an infestation of the skin, mucosa, orifices or natural cavities of humans or animals by larvae of flying dipterous insects that, at least during a certain period, feed from the live or necrotic tissue of the host in order to complete partial or total development of their evolutive cycle. 1 -5 It is an important problem in medical and veterinary science. Larvae or maggots are capable of infesting and inducing myiasis in skin wounds ('cutanaeous myiasis') or in the body ('deep myiasis') or complicate surgical wounds ('surgical myiasis'). They can invade human natural cavities such as the nasal fossae or eyes. They may penetrate further, causing urinary tract or gastrointestinal myiasis. 2 Infestation is rarely seen in the vulvar area. 4 Myiasis occurs all over the world, with more cases being reported in tropical, subtropical and warm temperate areas. 5 The incidence is probably under-estimated as it occurs principally in rural areas where cases are not registered.

The presence of suppurative lesions that attract and stimulate the deposit of eggs or larvae by female insects, as well as traumatic lesions, acquired immunodeficiency, comatose conditions, the habit of some populations of sitting on the ground, for example, during certain religious rites, precarious conditions of hygiene, principally among residents of rural areas and among people with psychiatric disturbances, and certain climatic conditions that favour the flies are all considered predisposing factors. 1,4,6 Fig. 1. Extensive areas of necrosis in the vulvar and perineal areas and one extracted larva.

Entomologically, the flies can be classified into three myiasis-producing groups: obligatory, facultative or accidental. 7 Obligatory myiasis, formerly known as biophagous, involves flies that are dependent on an animal host. The principal species are Cochliomyia hominivorax and Dermatobia hominis. The facultative or necrophagous organisms usually grow in dead or decomposing tissue. They may occasionally attack dead tissue in live organisms, in which they complete their life cycle. This group includes Sarcophaga sp. and C. macellaria, among others. Accidental or pseudomyiasis is caused by the ingestion of eggs of obligatory myiasis-producing flies, such as Hermetia illucens or Ceratitis capitata. 7 C. hominivorax is locally known as varejeira fly and is the principal cause of primary myiasis in the northeast of Brazil. In other countries, Wohlfahrtia magnifica has been reported as a cause of human vulvar myiasis, 5 and of vaginal myiasis in female camels. 8 In Brazil, to our knowledge, only one case of vulvar myiasis has been published. 4
